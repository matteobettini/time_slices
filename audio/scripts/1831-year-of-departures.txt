Eighteen thirty-one. A year when the old world begins to leave — and what comes next is uncharted.

In Paris, at the Salon of eighteen thirty-one, a painting stops visitors in their tracks. Eugène Delacroix has captured the July Revolution in a single image: a bare-breasted Marianne, tricolour in one hand, musket in the other, striding over corpses through the gunsmoke. Behind her, a motley army — workers in shirtsleeves, a bourgeois in a top hat, a street urchin with pistols. This is not classical dignity. This is chaos, passion, real death. Liberty Leading the People earns Delacroix the Legion of Honor. King Louis-Philippe buys it with state funds. Then he hides it for seventeen years. Even a revolution made safe for museums is still too dangerous for the walls.

Meanwhile, Victor Hugo is saving a building with a book. Notre-Dame de Paris, published in March, makes the cathedral its true protagonist — not the hunchback, but the stonework itself, described in loving architectural detail precisely because it was crumbling, vandalized, even slated for demolition. Hugo invents gothic revival literature to save gothic architecture. Within a decade, the restoration begins. Sometimes a novel can save a church.

In November, far from Paris, the greatest philosophical system of the age collapses. Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel dies in Berlin, likely of cholera, sixty-one years old and at the height of his fame. His philosophy — Spirit realising itself through history, thesis meeting antithesis in ever-higher synthesis — had seemed to explain everything. Now his students are already dividing into warring camps. The Right Hegelians will defend the Prussian state. The Young Hegelians will turn the master's tools against religion, hierarchy, and eventually capitalism itself. A young man named Karl Marx won't arrive in Berlin until eighteen thirty-six, but when he does, he'll find the interpretation wars already raging.

And in August, in Southampton County, Virginia, an enslaved preacher named Nat Turner leads the deadliest slave rebellion in American history. He has interpreted a solar eclipse as divine command. Over two days, Turner and his followers kill fifty-five white men, women, and children before being suppressed. The white retaliation is savage — over a hundred Black people murdered, most of them uninvolved. Turner hides for two months. He is hanged on November eleventh. The South responds with brutal new slave codes. The fragile peace of bondage has been exposed as a lie.

On December twenty-seventh, a different kind of departure. A twenty-two-year-old naturalist named Charles Darwin sails from Plymouth aboard HMS Beagle. The voyage is expected to last two years. It will take five. When he returns, he will carry notebooks full of observations that will reshape how humanity understands creation itself.

One system of explaining everything is ending. Another has not yet been born. Hegel's Spirit dies in November. Darwin's materialism won't be published for another twenty-eight years. In between: Delacroix painting revolution as myth, Hugo turning architecture into literature, Turner teaching America that its Enlightenment ideals and chattel slavery cannot peacefully coexist.

Eighteen thirty-one. The year of departures. The old certainties are leaving. What comes next is uncharted water.
