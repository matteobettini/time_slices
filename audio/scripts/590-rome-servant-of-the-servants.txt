February, five ninety. Pope Pelagius the Second is dying.

The plague that had devastated Constantinople for fifty years has finally reached Rome. The Tiber has flooded, destroying the city's granaries. The Lombards press from the north. And now the Pope coughs blood into his bed linens, another victim of the disease that will kill him within days.

Rome needs a leader. They turn to Gregory — former prefect of the city, now a monk who'd fled public life for contemplation. According to legend, he tried to escape, hiding in a basket carried by merchants through the city gates. But a miraculous column of light revealed his hiding place. Rome would have its Pope.

He didn't want it. But he accepted it. And in that acceptance, something fundamental shifts in Western history.

Gregory organises processions through the plague-stricken streets. The faithful march, praying for mercy. Eighty people collapse and die during one such march — the disease still hungry. Then, according to tradition, Gregory sees a vision: the Archangel Michael, standing atop Hadrian's ancient mausoleum, sheathing his sword. The plague is ending. The monument will forever after be called Castel Sant'Angelo — the Castle of the Holy Angel.

But Gregory's real monument is institutional. With Byzantine authority crumbling and no emperor to protect Rome, this reluctant Pope becomes administrator of the city's food supply, negotiator with barbarian kings, spiritual and temporal ruler in one. He calls himself servus servorum Dei — "servant of the servants of God." It's a title every Pope will use after him, and it captures something new: power as service, authority as humility.

He writes furiously. His Dialogues give us our only substantial account of Saint Benedict, founder of Western monasticism. His Moralia in Job becomes the standard commentary for centuries. His Pastoral Rule tells bishops how to care for souls. He sends missionaries to England, launching the conversion that will preserve classical learning through the darkest centuries.

Gregory is the last Roman and the first medieval man. Behind him, the ancient world collapses. Before him, the Middle Ages begin.

The servant of servants had become master of the West.
