Fifteen fifty-eight. The year of queens.

On November seventeenth, Mary Tudor dies at St. James's Palace. She is forty-two years old, exhausted by phantom pregnancies and the burden of trying to drag England back to Rome. Hours later, at Hatfield House in the countryside, her twenty-five-year-old half-sister receives the news. Elizabeth has been declared illegitimate twice, imprisoned in the Tower, suspected of treason. Now she is queen.

But Elizabeth inherits a kingdom divided by religion, drained by war, uncertain of its place in the world. And in Geneva, at that very moment, a Scottish preacher named John Knox has just published a polemic with a spectacular title: "The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women." His targets are the Catholic queens — Mary Tudor, Mary of Guise in Scotland. But his timing is catastrophic. Elizabeth takes the throne and never forgives him.

Knox's theological argument collapses against political reality. Elizabeth will reign for forty-five years while Knox remains permanently exiled from England.

Six weeks before Elizabeth's accession, another monarch had died. Charles the Fifth — Holy Roman Emperor, King of Spain, ruler of half the known world — passed away at the Monastery of Yuste in Spain. He had abdicated two years earlier, exhausted by decades of religious wars he could not win. His vast empire is already divided: Spain and the Netherlands to his son Philip, the imperial title to his brother Ferdinand.

The old order is fragmenting. The new one is uncertain.

In Venice, the elderly Titian paints his Crucifixion for Ancona — dark, emotionally devastating, stripped of the confident grandeur of his youth. In Paris, Joachim du Bellay publishes his sonnets mourning ancient Rome's ruins and his own disillusionment with papal corruption. Posthumously, Marguerite of Navarre's Heptaméron appears — seventy-two tales of love and betrayal in crisp French vernacular.

The connections run deep. Du Bellay mourns that empires fall and glory fades. Charles the Fifth retreats to a monastery, done with earthly power. Elizabeth embraces the cult of Gloriana, transforming limitation into spectacle. Knox thunders that women must not rule — and a woman rules for nearly half a century.

Fifteen fifty-eight. When the old certainties died, and the Elizabethan age began.
