Eighteen forty-three. The year dread found its philosopher.

In Copenhagen, a thirty-year-old Dane named Søren Kierkegaard is writing like a man possessed. In eight months, he publishes three books that will eventually reshape Western philosophy — though almost no one notices at the time. Either/Or appears in February, presenting the aesthetic and ethical lives as genuinely incompatible. No Hegelian synthesis. No comfortable resolution. Only choice.

Then, on October sixteenth, two more books arrive simultaneously. Fear and Trembling takes Abraham's sacrifice of Isaac as the ultimate test case. Faith, Kierkegaard argues, demands a leap beyond ethics itself — the teleological suspension of the ethical. It's terrifying. It's meant to be. His other book that day, Repetition, asks whether authentic selfhood can be recovered once lost — or only created anew.

Kierkegaard writes from personal wound. Two years earlier, he broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen, convinced some darkness in himself made marriage impossible. The ghost of that choice haunts every page. He publishes under pseudonyms — three different names for three books — because he believes indirect communication is the only honest approach to existential truth.

Meanwhile, in London, J.M.W. Turner exhibits two paintings that push toward pure abstraction. Light and Colour swirls yellow and gold into a vortex where Moses becomes barely visible, writing Genesis amid primal radiance. Its companion spirals into blue-black chaos. Turner is painting Goethe's colour theory — the idea that colour emerges from the interplay of light and darkness. Colour as emotion, not measurement. Form dissolving into pure feeling.

That same year, a twenty-four-year-old critic named John Ruskin publishes Modern Painters to defend Turner against those who dismiss his late work as formless blurs. Ruskin's manifesto of truth to nature will define Victorian aesthetics.

And on December nineteenth, Charles Dickens publishes A Christmas Carol. Six thousand copies sell out by Christmas Eve. Scrooge's ghostly visitation is really about industrial capitalism — the chains Marley forged link by link, the workhouses Scrooge endorses, Tiny Tim's preventable death. Ghost story as social critique.

Thomas Carlyle attacks the same industrial England more directly in Past and Present, comparing a medieval monastery's organic community to modern society's cash nexus — money as the only remaining bond between humans.

While in London, John Stuart Mill publishes A System of Logic, codifying empiricism's methods. Where Kierkegaard plunges into subjective truth, Mill builds the framework for objective investigation.

Eighteen forty-three. The year philosophy discovered that existence precedes essence — that the individual stands alone, anxious, before infinite possibility. Kierkegaard called it Angst. We still live in its shadow.
