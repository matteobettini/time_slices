Fifteen seventy-one. Blood on the Wine-Dark Sea.

On October seventh, over four hundred galleys clash in the Gulf of Patras — the last great battle of oared ships. The Holy League faces the Ottoman fleet. Don John of Austria commands two hundred and six Christian galleys against Ali Pasha's armada.

The battle is decided in hours. Forty thousand Ottoman casualties. Two hundred and thirty ships captured or destroyed. Twelve thousand Christian galley slaves freed from their oars. The myth of Ottoman naval invincibility shatters in a single afternoon.

Venice celebrates. The great triumvirate of painters — Titian, Tintoretto, and Veronese — respond with color as triumph. Veronese paints Agostino Barbarigo, the Venetian commander who died in the battle, clutching a Turkish arrow. Tintoretto begins his monumental Battle of Lepanto.

But in Milan that September, a boy is born who will overturn everything these masters achieved. His name is Michelangelo Merisi. He will take the name of his family's town: Caravaggio. His radical chiaroscuro — figures emerging from darkness into spotlight intensity — will demolish the golden luminosity Venice perfected.

At Lepanto itself, a twenty-three-year-old Spanish soldier named Miguel de Cervantes takes three harquebus wounds. One cripples his left hand forever. He will later call Lepanto "the most noble and memorable event that past centuries have seen." The hand he loses is not his writing hand. The Quixote survives.

On December twenty-seventh, in the Lutheran town of Weil der Stadt, Johannes Kepler is born. His laws of planetary motion will prove the heavens don't move in perfect circles — overthrowing the very cosmology the Church celebrates.

The future masters of darkness and motion take their first breaths. Cervantes, wounded. Caravaggio, crying. Kepler, arriving. Victory creates the confidence of the Spanish Golden Age. The men shaped by this battle will define the century to come.

Fifteen seventy-one — when Christendom stopped retreating and the Baroque began.
