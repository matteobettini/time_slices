Eighteen sixty-three. The year everything gets stripped bare.

In May, in Paris, the Emperor himself orders an exhibition of rejected art. The official Salon has turned away over two thousand paintings, and the protests have grown loud enough that Napoleon the Third decides to let the public judge for themselves. The Salon des Refusés opens — and visitors crowd in, laughing, scandalized, mocking.

But in one room hangs a painting that will haunt the century.

Édouard Manet's Luncheon on the Grass shows a naked woman seated on the forest floor with two fully dressed men. She is not Venus, not Diana, not any mythological figure. She is contemporary. She is looking directly at you. And she does not care that she is naked.

The scandal isn't the nudity. Paris has seen plenty of nudes. The scandal is the refusal to idealize. This woman exists in the same world as the viewer — and she meets your gaze without apology. Manet has committed the modern sin: showing things as they are.

That same month, Charles Baudelaire publishes his manifesto in Le Figaro. The Painter of Modern Life argues that beauty is no longer eternal — it is transient, fleeting, contingent. The artist's job is to capture the present as it vanishes.

In August, Eugène Delacroix dies — the last great Romantic master. The baton passes to a generation that believes in neither classical ideals nor Romantic dreams, only in the shock of the actual.

Three thousand miles away, another kind of unmasking is happening. On the first of January, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation took effect, declaring slaves in rebel states forever free. The Civil War is no longer about union — it's about slavery. In July, the Battle of Gettysburg turns the tide of the war. And in November, at the dedication of a cemetery, Lincoln delivers two hundred seventy-two words that redefine what America means.

A nation conceived in Liberty. Dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. The entire founding compromise with slavery — undone in a single speech.

Meanwhile, in Paris, another scandal. Ernest Renan's Life of Jesus becomes an instant bestseller by doing to Christ what Manet did to the nude: stripping away the divine alibi. Jesus is presented as a charming Galilean teacher, admirable, influential — but human. No miracles. No resurrection. Just history.

The Catholic Church puts the book on the Index. Renan is expelled from his professorship. But the book sells and sells and sells. Biblical criticism has reached the masses.

And in London, that same January, the world's first underground railway opens. Passengers descend into the earth, travel by gaslight through tunnels beneath the city, emerge on the other side. Romance gives way to efficiency. The commuter is born.

Everything connects through demystification. Manet shows the nude without classical pretense. Lincoln strips America of its founding lie. Renan strips Christ of divinity. Mill — whose Utilitarianism appears this year — strips ethics of divine command. The modern world is arriving, and its signature gesture is to remove the veil.

To show things as they are. Not as tradition dressed them.

Eighteen sixty-three. The year modernity announced itself — naked, democratic, godless — and dared you to look away.
