Paris, nineteen forty-three. Nazi tanks patrol the boulevards. Curfews empty the streets by dusk. And in this strangest of circumstances, freedom is being redefined.

In a studio on the Rue des Grands-Augustins, Pablo Picasso spends a year preparing for a single afternoon. He draws sketch after sketch of a man holding a lamb. Then, in one furious session, he sculpts L'Homme au Mouton — Man with a Sheep. Not a gentle shepherd, but something rawer: the brutal fact of protection in a world of slaughter. Banned from exhibiting, labeled degenerate by the Nazis, Picasso refuses to leave Paris. His presence is resistance.

That June, at the Théâtre de la Cité, Jean-Paul Sartre's play The Flies premieres. The ancient Greek myth of Orestes becomes a parable of liberation. A hero returns to a city paralyzed by collective guilt and frees it by accepting responsibility for his actions. The German censors approve the production. They don't understand what they're watching.

But the real revolution comes from Gallimard publishing house: seven hundred pages of phenomenological ontology titled Being and Nothingness. Sartre's masterwork argues that existence precedes essence — we are not born with a fixed nature, but create ourselves through choices. He writes provocatively that the French have never been so free as under German occupation. When every decision carries absolute weight, there are no excuses. No appeals to duty. Only radical freedom.

In April, on the eve of Passover, Jews in the Warsaw Ghetto mount the first armed urban uprising against Nazi occupation. Seven hundred fighters with pistols and homemade bombs against tanks and flamethrowers. They hold out for nearly a month. The uprising fails militarily, but its meaning transcends defeat: victims can become actors. Resistance is possible.

Albert Camus, meanwhile, edits the underground newspaper Combat by night. His journalism calls for a liberal revolution. His Letters to a German Friend circulate in secret, explaining why one must resist even knowing the boulder will roll back down.

Nineteen forty-three condenses existentialism from seminar topic to lived reality. Sartre writes that we are our choices. This year forces choices with lethal stakes: collaborate or resist, speak or stay silent, hide the persecuted or look away. The occupation makes philosophy concrete. Freedom is not an abstraction. It's a daily decision. And bad faith can cost lives.
