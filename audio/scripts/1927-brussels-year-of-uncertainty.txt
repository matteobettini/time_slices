Nineteen twenty-seven. The year certainty died.

In a conference room in Brussels, twenty-nine of the greatest minds in physics gather for what may be history's most consequential debate. Albert Einstein, stubborn defender of cosmic order, faces Niels Bohr across the table. The subject: quantum mechanics. Einstein insists the universe must be deterministic. God does not play dice. Bohr quietly demolishes him. Stop telling God what to do.

Werner Heisenberg has just published his uncertainty principle. You cannot know both where a particle is and where it's going — not because your instruments are crude, but because reality itself resists complete knowledge. The act of observing changes what you observe. Classical physics depended on the premise that nature exists independently of the observer. That premise is now dead.

In Germany, Martin Heidegger publishes Being and Time, asking a question philosophy had somehow forgotten: what does it mean to be? His answer is strange and profound. Human existence — Dasein — is fundamentally temporal. We are thrown into time, project ourselves toward death, and find authentic existence only by confronting our finitude. Certainty was never available to creatures like us.

Meanwhile in Paris, the final volume of Marcel Proust's search appears posthumously. Time Regained reveals what the entire vast novel was building toward: involuntary memory defeats death. The taste of a madeleine recovers a whole world. Virginia Woolf publishes To the Lighthouse, where the middle section compresses ten years and a world war into twenty pages of empty rooms. Time passes. We are swept along.

In Berlin, Fritz Lang's Metropolis imagines a future where workers become extensions of machines. In Hollywood, The Jazz Singer speaks — actually speaks — and silent cinema dies overnight. René Magritte unveils paintings where men in bowler hats float serenely and assassins pose beside corpses. Reality can no longer be trusted.

And on May twentieth, a young pilot named Charles Lindbergh takes off from New York. Thirty-three hours later, alone, he lands in Paris. The Atlantic — that vast impossible distance — suddenly seems crossable.

What connects these scattered moments? A shared discovery: that the observer is entangled with the observed. The confident positivism of the nineteenth century — the belief that everything could be measured, predicted, known — that faith is finished. Art, literature, and physics all converge on the same unsettling truth.

We cannot stand outside the world to see it clearly. We are inside it, shaping what we see by the act of looking.

At Solvay, when the vote is taken, Bohr wins. The Copenhagen interpretation becomes orthodoxy. Einstein will never accept it, but the twentieth century proceeds without his approval.

We live now in the world that nineteen twenty-seven made — uncertain, observer-dependent, always already in time.
