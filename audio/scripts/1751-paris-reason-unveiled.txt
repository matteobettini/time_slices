Seventeen fifty-one. Paris.

On the twenty-eighth of June, a book appears that will terrify kings and priests across Europe. Its title is dry: the Encyclopédie, or Reasoned Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts, and Crafts. Its ambition is revolutionary: to compile all human knowledge and make it available to anyone who can read.

Denis Diderot edits this vast machine. Jean le Rond d'Alembert writes its philosophical manifesto, the Preliminary Discourse. All knowledge, d'Alembert argues, begins in sensation — not in divine revelation. Memory gives us history, reason gives us philosophy, imagination gives us art. No room here for mysteries beyond human understanding.

The Church and Crown recognise the threat immediately. But the first volume sells out. The engine of Enlightenment has started.

Meanwhile, Voltaire publishes his own revolution: Le Siècle de Louis XIV. History, he insists, is not just the chronicle of kings and battles — it is the story of civilisation itself. Arts, sciences, manners, institutions. He identifies four great ages of humanity: Periclean Athens, Augustan Rome, Renaissance Italy, and the France of Louis the Fourteenth. This is cultural history before the term exists.

Across the Channel, Thomas Gray publishes a quieter masterpiece. His Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard meditates on death — not the death of kings, but of ordinary people. "Full many a flower is born to blush unseen," he writes, "and waste its sweetness on the desert air." The rude forefathers of the hamlet deserve remembrance too.

And in the salons of Paris, François Boucher paints Venus rising from her toilette for Madame de Pompadour — the king's mistress, but also the philosophes' most powerful protector. She encourages the Encyclopédie. She employs Boucher. She shields Voltaire.

What connects all of this? A conviction that human happiness matters, that knowledge should circulate freely, that the old authorities have had their day. The philosophes have declared war on ignorance.

In four years, the Lisbon earthquake will test their optimism. But for now, reason stands unveiled, radiating light.
