Imagine a king who nearly lost everything. In eight seventy-eight, Alfred of Wessex hid in the marshes of Somerset while Viking armies swept across his kingdom. Now, just twelve years later, that same king sits in Winchester translating philosophy.

Not copying it. Translating it. Taking Boethius's Latin Consolation of Philosophy — the meditation of a condemned Roman senator awaiting execution — and rendering it into English. Anglo-Saxon English. The language of farmers and warriors, being used for the first time to discuss divine providence and the problem of evil.

Alfred's programme is revolutionary. He gathers scholars from Wales, Mercia, the Frankish continent. They translate Gregory the Great's Pastoral Care, the foundational text on how to lead a Christian community. To each bishop, Alfred sends a copy — with a jeweled pointer worth fifty coins, for following the words as you read.

One such pointer may survive. The Alfred Jewel, discovered in Somerset in sixteen ninety-three, bears the inscription: Alfred ordered me made. Gold, enamel, rock crystal — astonishingly precious, because learning was worth it.

In Ireland, monks at Armagh illuminate the Mac Durnan Gospels, continuing the insular tradition that first brought Christianity to the English. Viking raids have scattered the scriptoria, but the art endures. Those interlaced serpents and spiraling knotwork — visual prayers that need no translation.

Meanwhile, Guthrum is dying. The Viking warlord who nearly destroyed Wessex, defeated at Edington, baptised with Alfred as godfather, has ruled East Anglia for twelve years as the Christian king Æthelstan. His death marks the end of an era. The enemies are becoming neighbours.

Alfred insists that all freeborn boys learn to read English before Latin. Knowledge, he declares, should not be locked in an elite language. The democratisation of learning begins here, in a kingdom that nearly perished.

When you read English today, remember Winchester.
