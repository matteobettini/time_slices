Nineteen twenty-nine. The year the eye was sliced.

In June, a Parisian audience watches as Luis Buñuel draws a razor across a woman's eyeball. The film is Un Chien Andalou—An Andalusian Dog—and nothing that follows makes any more sense than its title. Ants crawl from hands. Dead donkeys drape across pianos. Dreams become cinema. Salvador Dalí and Buñuel have built their screenplay from pure unconscious imagery, and Surrealism has found its perfect weapon.

But Surrealism isn't the only assault on reason that year.

In March, at a sanatorium in Davos, Switzerland, two German philosophers stage the century's defining intellectual duel. Ernst Cassirer defends the Enlightenment dream: through language, through science, through symbolic forms, humanity reaches toward the infinite. Martin Heidegger says no—we are finite beings, thrown into time, defined by our confrontation with death. The young Emmanuel Levinas watches from the audience, feeling as though he's witnessing creation and apocalypse at once.

In September, the Vienna Circle publishes its manifesto. Philosophy should be scientific, they declare. Any statement that cannot be verified is meaningless. Metaphysics is nonsense. The logical positivists want to clean up thought itself.

And then, in October, the markets crash.

On Black Tuesday—October twenty-ninth—sixteen million shares trade hands on the New York Stock Exchange. The ticker tape runs hours behind the chaos. Fourteen billion dollars vanish in a single day. The Roaring Twenties die on a trading floor in lower Manhattan. The Great Depression begins.

Nine days later, the Museum of Modern Art opens its doors—three visionary women betting that when everything else fails, art still matters.

Reason against unreason. That's the year's secret theme. The Vienna Circle demands verification while Dalí insists dreams reveal truth. Heidegger embraces anxiety as authentic existence while Wall Street discovers that collective behavior can be as irrational as any nightmare.

William Faulkner publishes The Sound and the Fury—four narrators, three of them unreliable, one of them barely conscious. The title quotes Shakespeare: life as a tale told by an idiot, signifying nothing.

And perhaps that's nineteen twenty-nine's message. The idiot's tale and the logical proposition. The sliced eye and the stock ticker. All of them reaching for meaning in a world that may not have any to offer.

The breaking point, where the twentieth century truly begins.
