July fourteenth, seventeen eighty-nine. A Parisian crowd storms the Bastille, seeking gunpowder to defend the revolution. When they breach the walls, they find only seven prisoners — four forgers, two madmen, and a count imprisoned at his family's request. The revolution has stormed a symbol, not a dungeon. But symbols, it turns out, can change the world.

At the Paris Salon that summer, Jacques-Louis David unveils a painting so politically charged that authorities tried to suppress it. The Lictors Bring to Brutus the Bodies of His Sons shows the founder of the Roman Republic who condemned his own sons for conspiring to restore the monarchy. Brutus sits in shadow, stoic, while his wife and daughters wail in grief. The message is unmistakable: republican virtue demands any sacrifice. After the Bastille falls, the painting becomes prophecy.

Meanwhile in London, William Blake is revolutionising art in a different way. His Songs of Innocence fuses poetry and image through "illuminated printing" — each copy hand-coloured, unique. The Lamb asks who made thee? The Chimney Sweeper shows a child sold into servitude dreaming of angels. Blake trusts imagination over reason, vision over calculation — a gentle dissent from the Age of Reason.

But across the Atlantic, another voice emerges. Olaudah Equiano publishes his autobiography, the first widely-read account by an African describing the horror of the Middle Passage. Kidnapped at eleven from his Nigerian village, enslaved in the Caribbean and Virginia, Equiano eventually purchased his freedom. His narrative transforms the abolition movement by giving the slave trade a human face.

In August, the National Assembly adopts the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Drafted by Lafayette with Thomas Jefferson's assistance, seventeen articles declare that men are born free, sovereignty resides in the nation, and liberty is limited only by harm to others. Enlightenment philosophy has become law.

That same year, Jeremy Bentham publishes his foundational work on utilitarianism. Morality, he argues, reduces to a calculus: the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Where Kant grounded ethics in pure reason, Bentham grounds it in consequences — a secular framework for social reform.

The Declaration promised universal rights. But Equiano's narrative asks the uncomfortable question: if all men are born free, what of those in chains? The tension between revolutionary ideals and revolutionary violence will tear France apart. Philosophy has taken power. Now it must learn what that costs.
