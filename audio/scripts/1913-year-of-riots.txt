The year nineteen thirteen. The year of riots.

On the evening of May twenty-ninth, Parisian society fills the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées for a new ballet by Diaghilev's Ballets Russes. The program says: The Rite of Spring. What they don't know is that everything they understand about music, about dance, about beauty itself, is about to be demolished.

Stravinsky's score erupts — brutal, pounding, dissonant. Nijinsky's dancers don't glide; they stomp. Flat-footed, bent-kneed, primitive. The audience begins to hiss. Then to shout. Then to throw punches.

The noise grows so loud the dancers can't hear the music. Nijinsky stands in the wings, screaming the counts. Police are called at intermission. Diaghilev, they say, just smiled: exactly what I wanted.

Three months earlier, in New York, a different kind of riot. The Armory Show introduces America to Cubism, Fauvism, the European avant-garde. One painting becomes the lightning rod: Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase. Critics call it an explosion in a shingle factory. The public is offered prizes to find the lady in the picture.

The painting had been rejected by the Cubists themselves as too Futurist. Now it makes Duchamp famous in a country he's never visited.

Meanwhile, in November, a forty-one-year-old asthmatic named Marcel Proust publishes a novel at his own expense. André Gide at the NRF had rejected it. The book is called Swann's Way. The opening pages describe a madeleine dipped in tea — and literature discovers that memory can defeat time.

In Germany, Edmund Husserl publishes his Ideas, introducing phenomenology's radical method: bracketing reality itself to investigate pure consciousness. His students at Göttingen include a young man named Martin Heidegger.

And in the Balkans, the Second Balkan War erupts. Bulgaria attacks its former allies. By August, new treaties redraw the map. Europe calls it the powder keg. August nineteen fourteen is fourteen months away.

The year's theme? Simultaneity as assault. Stravinsky attacks the body. Duchamp attacks the eye. Husserl attacks the mind. All share a method: making the familiar strange.

The audiences riot because they recognize the threat. Something is ending. Something is beginning. The twentieth century is learning to see.
