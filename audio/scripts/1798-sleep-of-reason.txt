Seventeen ninety-eight. The year reason wakes to find it has become something else.

In Madrid, Francisco Goya hunches over copper plates, etching nightmares. Plate forty-three will become the image of an age: an artist slumped at his desk while bats and owls swarm from the darkness behind him. "El sueño de la razón produce monstruos" — the sleep of reason produces monsters. But what does it mean? Is this a warning that without reason, chaos emerges? Or a confession that reason itself — the Enlightenment's great weapon — has become the nightmare?

Goya leaves the question open. He has seen the guillotine's logic.

In Vienna, a different vision. Joseph Haydn, sixty-six years old, unveils his masterpiece: The Creation. When the chorus sings "Let there be light" and the orchestra explodes from whispered darkness to blazing C major, audiences weep. Divine order still holds — or can at least be imagined. Goya's monsters and Haydn's angels respond to the same revolutionary decade.

Meanwhile, in a Bristol bookshop, a small anonymous volume appears: Lyrical Ballads. William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge have quietly detonated English poetry. Wordsworth writes of leech-gatherers and abandoned mothers in "the real language of men." Coleridge offers the supernatural — a spectral ship, a dead albatross, guilt and redemption on a painted ocean. The Preface will come later; the revolution happens here first, in practice.

And in London, a clergyman named Thomas Malthus publishes an essay that will haunt two centuries. Population grows geometrically; food grows arithmetically. Famine is mathematics. Malthus writes explicitly against the utopian hopes of Condorcet — the philosopher who died in a revolutionary prison still believing in human perfectibility. Where the Enlightenment promised progress, Malthus promises catastrophe. Darwin will later credit him with the key to natural selection.

On the nineteenth of May, Napoleon sails from Toulon. Thirty-eight thousand soldiers — and one hundred sixty-seven scholars. Mathematicians, naturalists, artists. The Egyptian campaign is war and encyclopedia combined. Next July, they will find the Rosetta Stone. Enlightenment science becomes imperial conquest.

In Ireland, Wolfe Tone and the United Irishmen rise — and fall. Revolution spreads and fails. Empires adapt.

What connects these fragments? The exhaustion of pure reason. Goya's sleeping artist, Malthus's demographic doom, Wordsworth's retreat to nature, Schelling's search for the World Soul — all respond to the same question. The Terror has happened. Reason promised liberation and delivered the guillotine. Now what?

Romanticism is not reason's opposite. It is reason's exhausted child — dreaming of monsters, dreaming of light.

Seventeen ninety-eight: the year philosophy became population control, poetry became plain speech, and an artist fell asleep to find the monsters waiting.
