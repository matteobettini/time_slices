The year fourteen thirty-one. A nineteen-year-old peasant girl stands in Rouen's marketplace, flames rising around her.

Her name is Jeanne d'Arc — Joan of Arc. Two years earlier, she had appeared before the Dauphin of France, claiming saints' voices commanded her mission. She lifted the siege of Orléans. She crowned a king at Reims. She changed the course of a war that had ground on for nearly a century.

Now she burns.

Her English captors and their Burgundian allies have orchestrated everything. Bishop Pierre Cauchon presides over a trial designed from the start to produce one verdict: heresy. Seventy sessions of interrogation. Questions designed to trap her. Asked if she knows she is in God's grace — a theological trap, since claiming certainty could itself be presumption — she answers with devastating clarity: "If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me there." The clergy are stunned. It is theologically flawless.

But theology was never the point. Politics was.

On May thirtieth, they tie her to a stake. Her ashes will be scattered into the Seine — no relics, no grave, nothing for followers to venerate. She dies calling on Christ.

Simultaneously, a different kind of judgment unfolds. In Basel, the Council has just opened — the most ambitious assertion yet that the Church as a whole holds authority above the pope. The question haunting Basel is the same one Rouen asked: who judges? Who holds the keys? A young scholar named Nicholas of Cusa is preparing to join; his later philosophy of "learned ignorance" will question whether anyone can grasp divine truth fully.

Meanwhile, the artists work. In Ghent, Jan van Eyck labors over the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb — oil glazes building luminosity impossible in older techniques, rendering jewels and velvet and skin with almost hallucinatory precision. In Florence, Fra Angelico paints the Last Judgment: Christ enthroned in white light, the saved ascending through a garden, the damned dragged into flame.

Both painters seek to make the invisible visible. To render divine truth undeniably seen.

And in Paris, a child is born. His name will become François Villon — the greatest French poet of the century, whose verses will speak of taverns and thieves and gallows, who will write his own testament in the shadow of execution. Born as Joan dies. Heir to a world of flames and tribunals. He will laugh at it all — the bitter laughter of someone who knows judgment firsthand.

Fourteen thirty-one. Judgment and flame. The old order tries a saint as a heretic. The question of who interprets the sacred has no safe answer. Only consequences.
