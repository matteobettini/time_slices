Ten sixty-six. The year England changes forever.

It begins with a death and ends with a conquest. In January, Edward the Confessor dies childless in Westminster. The throne sits empty — and three men reach for it.

Harold Godwinson, the most powerful earl in England, claims Edward named him heir on his deathbed. The Witan — the council of nobles — agrees. Harold is crowned within days.

But across the Channel, William of Normandy sees things differently. He insists Edward promised him the crown years ago. More damning: he claims Harold himself once swore an oath — hand on holy relics — to support William's claim. An oath now broken.

William does something extraordinary. He appeals to Rome. Pope Alexander the Second grants him a papal banner. Invasion becomes holy crusade.

In the north, Harald Hardrada of Norway lands with three hundred ships. Harold marches his army two hundred miles in four days and destroys the Norwegian threat at Stamford Bridge. It is one of history's great forced marches.

Three days later, William lands in the south.

Harold turns his exhausted men around and races back. On October fourteenth, at a place called Senlac Hill near Hastings, the two armies meet.

The English fight on foot, locked in their famous shield wall. The Normans attack on horseback, with archers softening the line. For hours, the wall holds. But as daylight fades, something happens. Tradition says an arrow strikes Harold in the eye. The English line breaks. By nightfall, a kingdom has fallen.

What follows is total transformation.

The Bayeux Tapestry — seventy meters of embroidery — tells the story from the Norman perspective. But here's the strange thing: it was stitched by Anglo-Saxon needleworkers, probably in Canterbury. The conquered making propaganda for their conquerors.

William imports Norman lords, Norman bishops, Norman architecture. Within decades, the White Tower rises over London — massive stone walls, round arches, a fortress announcing that this new order builds to last.

And the language fractures. French becomes the tongue of court and law. English retreats to the fields. Over two centuries, they merge into something new. This is why we have "commence" and "begin," "royal" and "kingly." English becomes a language doubled.

Ten sixty-six. The arrow. The oath. The conquest. The year England becomes something new.
