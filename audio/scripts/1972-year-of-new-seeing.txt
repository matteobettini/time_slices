Nineteen seventy-two. The year a razor sliced the eye.

In Kassel, Germany, a Swiss curator named Harald Szeemann opens documenta five — and rewrites the rules of what art can be. Joseph Beuys sets up an office for direct democracy, staffing it himself, debating visitors about politics while calling the whole thing sculpture. Gilbert and George stand motionless as living artworks. Daniel Buren covers everything in stripes and dares you to call it decoration.

Szeemann's concept is "Individual Mythologies" — the artist as their own private universe. It's a declaration: art can be pure idea. The object is optional.

Meanwhile, the BBC is broadcasting something equally revolutionary to living rooms across Britain. John Berger, novelist and critic, presents Ways of Seeing — four half-hour episodes that transform how we understand images. Seeing, Berger argues, is never innocent. The old masters painted women for male pleasure; advertising descends directly from oil painting's celebration of possessions. That Renaissance nude and this magazine advertisement are cousins. Both teach you to desire what you don't have.

In Paris, Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari publish Anti-Oedipus — their explosive attack on Freud. The Oedipus complex, they argue, isn't a universal truth about human psychology. It's a mechanism of control, teaching us to internalize the family structure capitalism requires. Desire isn't lack — it's production. The unconscious isn't a theater replaying family dramas. It's a factory making reality.

Their question haunts the post-sixty-eight moment: if revolution failed, maybe we haven't understood what we're fighting against.

That same year, Italo Calvino publishes Invisible Cities — Marco Polo describing fifty-five impossible cities to Kublai Khan. Cities of memory, cities of desire, cities of the dead. Each exists only in its telling. Postmodern literature's purest expression: description creates reality.

And in the real world, the map itself is tearing. On February twenty-first, Richard Nixon shakes Mao Zedong's hand in Beijing — thirty years of Cold War assumptions rewritten in a single gesture. On June seventeenth, five burglars are caught inside the Watergate complex. On September fifth, Palestinian terrorists take Israeli athletes hostage at the Munich Olympics. The world is watching. Eleven Israelis die.

Nineteen seventy-two. The year we learned that how we see determines what we can imagine — politically, aesthetically, psychologically. The old ways of seeing were under assault. New ones were being born.
