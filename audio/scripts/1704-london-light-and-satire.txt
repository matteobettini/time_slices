Seventeen oh four. Light and Satire.

In London, Isaac Newton publishes Opticks — his second masterpiece, written in English rather than Latin. Where the Principia had revealed the mathematics of motion, Opticks reveals the mathematics of light.

Newton's experiments are precise, repeatable, devastating. He passes sunlight through a prism and watches it separate into colours — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Each colour bends at a different angle. Each has a measurable property. White light, he demonstrates, is not pure but composite — a mixture that can be unmixed.

This overturns two thousand years of optical theory. Since Aristotle, philosophers had assumed that white light was fundamental and that colours arose from contamination, from mixture with darkness. Newton proves the opposite: colour is the nature of light, white is the mixture.

He waits to publish until Robert Hooke dies in seventeen oh three. Hooke had challenged Newton's optical theories for decades. The dead cannot object.

In the same year, Jonathan Swift publishes A Tale of a Tub anonymously. It's a savage parody of religious enthusiasm and intellectual pretension. Three brothers — Peter, Martin, Jack — inherit coats from their father, along with a will forbidding any alterations. The coats represent Christian practice; the will represents Scripture. The brothers alter their coats endlessly, each claiming to follow the original.

Peter adds shoulder-knots, gold lace, embroidery — the accretions of Catholic tradition. Jack tears his coat to shreds trying to restore its original simplicity — Puritan iconoclasm. Martin, the Anglican middle way, picks his seams more carefully.

But Swift's digressions overwhelm his tale. He parodies the "Modern" authors who believe they have surpassed the Ancients, the projectors and systematizers whose confidence outstrips their competence. Queen Anne finds it blasphemous. It costs Swift any hope of episcopal preferment.

On the thirteenth of August, the Duke of Marlborough and Prince Eugene of Savoy crush the Franco-Bavarian army at Blenheim. It is England's greatest continental victory since Agincourt. Marshal Tallard is captured; thirty thousand French and Bavarian troops are killed, wounded, or taken prisoner. The myth of French invincibility under Louis the Fourteenth — shattered.

Days earlier, an Anglo-Dutch fleet captures Gibraltar. A strategic afterthought that becomes permanent.

On the twenty-eighth of October, John Locke dies at Oates in Essex. The founding empiricist — who argued that the mind begins as a blank slate, that all knowledge derives from experience — leaves his revolution unfinished.

In Hanover, Leibniz completes his New Essays on Human Understanding — a systematic refutation of Locke's empiricism. Innate ideas exist, Leibniz argues, like veins in marble: the form precedes the sculptor's chisel. But when Locke dies, Leibniz withholds publication. It seems unseemly to attack a man who cannot reply. The New Essays won't appear until seventeen sixty-five.

The year's method is decomposition. Newton takes apart white light. Swift takes apart religious tradition. Marlborough takes apart the French army. Locke built knowledge from simple ideas; Leibniz will build the counterargument — but only after Locke is dust.

Seventeen oh four. The Enlightenment sharpens its instruments. Break it apart. See how it works. Rebuild from first principles.
