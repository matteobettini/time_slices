Paris, nineteen thirty-seven. The most spectacular confrontation between civilization and its own extinction.

At the International Exposition, a small pavilion stands as an indictment disguised as architecture. The Spanish Republic — besieged by Franco's fascists, abandoned by democracies — has sent its artists. Inside hangs a gray canvas twenty-five feet wide. No color. No heroes. Just fragments of screaming horses, dismembered bodies, a mother clutching her dead child, and at the center, a harsh electric lightbulb illuminating the horror.

Pablo Picasso painted Guernica in thirty-five days — roughly the same time it took German bombers to reduce that Basque market town to rubble. April twenty-sixth: the Condor Legion's planes appeared over a Monday market. They dropped incendiaries, then returned to machine-gun survivors fleeing through the fields. Sixteen hundred civilians killed or wounded. Eighty-five percent of the town destroyed. Hitler testing his weapons. Franco accepting the gift.

Across the Trocadéro, Albert Speer's German Pavilion faces Boris Iofan's Soviet tower — two totalitarian giants in frozen combat, their muscular sculptures reaching skyward like prayers to different gods of history. Between them, visitors stroll past demonstrations of modernity, progress, technology. The same technology that could level ancient cities in an afternoon.

In a Roman prison, Antonio Gramsci is dying. After eleven years of fascist captivity, his body broken, he leaves notebooks that pose the essential question: how do ruling classes maintain power? Not through force alone, he writes, but through the manufacture of consent — through culture, education, common sense itself. His concept of hegemony will reshape Marxist thought for generations.

Meanwhile, across the Atlantic, Zora Neale Hurston publishes a novel her Harlem Renaissance peers dismiss as trivial. Their Eyes Were Watching God — the story of Janie Crawford discovering that horizons are "the beginning of everything." A declaration of imaginative freedom precisely when actual horizons were closing across Europe.

And in a Oxford study, J.R.R. Tolkien sends his hobbits into a world of dark lords and corrupted power. The Hobbit appears in September — escape literature, perhaps, but escape into precisely the anxieties the newspapers were reporting daily.

Nineteen thirty-seven assembles the materials for understanding how civilization collapses — and how cultural work might resist, record, or imaginatively transcend that collapse. The bombed town, the dying republic, the imprisoned philosopher, the exiled artist. Guernica's lightbulb still burns: illuminating atrocity, insisting on witness.

The catastrophe was not coming. It had already arrived.
