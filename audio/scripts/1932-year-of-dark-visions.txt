Nineteen thirty-two. The year everything darkens.

The Great Depression has reached its nadir. In America, one in four workers has no job. In Germany, the number is one in three. Hunger lines stretch around city blocks. Shantytowns sprawl on the edges of every major city. They call them Hoovervilles — named for the president who promised prosperity and delivered catastrophe.

In summer, forty-three thousand World War One veterans march on Washington. They call themselves the Bonus Army. They want early payment of service certificates — money promised for the war they survived, not due until nineteen forty-five. They set up camp on the Anacostia Flats, sleeping in makeshift shelters, waiting for Congress to act.

Congress says no. President Hoover orders the Army to clear them out. General Douglas MacArthur leads cavalry and tanks against unarmed veterans. They burn the shelters. Women and children flee tear gas. Major Dwight Eisenhower watches and calls it a pitiful scene.

In November, Franklin Roosevelt wins in a landslide, promising something new. In Germany, Hitler runs for president and loses — but his party becomes the largest in the Reichstag. The machinery is in place.

The art of this year refuses consolation.

In Dresden, Otto Dix completes The War — a triptych painted on wooden panels like a Renaissance altarpiece. But instead of Christ's suffering, we see soldiers marching into fog, skeletal remains rotting in trenches, a gas-masked figure haunting a devastated landscape. Dix served in the Great War. He knows what he paints.

In Detroit, Frida Kahlo lies on a hospital bed after a miscarriage. She paints what happened: her naked body, six symbolic objects floating around her, connected by red filaments like umbilical cords. The fetus. The pelvic bone. The industrial machinery of Ford's factory on the horizon. She transforms private trauma into public testimony.

And in literature, two visions of nightmare.

Aldous Huxley publishes Brave New World — a dystopia not of punishment but of pleasure. Humans decanted in laboratories, sorted into castes, pacified with a drug called soma. Everyone is happy. Everyone is dead inside. It's a parody of H.G. Wells's techno-optimism, written while the Depression proves industrial civilization can collapse.

In Paris, Louis-Ferdinand Céline publishes Journey to the End of the Night. His hero Ferdinand Bardamu wanders from the trenches of the Great War to colonial Africa to Ford's assembly lines, finding absurdity everywhere. The prose is slang-soaked, nihilistic, brutal. It detonates French literature.

Both novels say the same thing: modernity has perfected its mechanisms of control. Whether through pleasure or exploitation, escape requires seeing through the spectacle.

And what does philosophy offer? Henri Bergson, seventy-three years old, publishes his final major work: The Two Sources of Morality and Religion. He distinguishes closed morality — tribal, defensive — from open morality, which springs from mystical experience and extends love to all humanity. Only the complete mystic, he writes, can break through to genuine moral freedom.

It's Bergson's answer to the catastrophe unfolding around him: neither reason nor instinct will save civilization. Only creative love.

The Vienna Circle positivists, meanwhile, are watching the darkness close in. Soon they will flee, or be silenced.

Nineteen thirty-two. The year of dark visions. The veterans march and are driven out. The artists paint what they see. The writers diagnose a sick civilization.

And somewhere in Munich, a failed painter waits for his moment.
