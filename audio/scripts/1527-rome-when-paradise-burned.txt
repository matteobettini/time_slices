Rome, fifteen twenty-seven. The sixth of May.

Twenty thousand soldiers breach the walls of the Eternal City. They are German Landsknechts, Spanish veterans, Italian mercenaries — hungry, unpaid for months, and leaderless. Their commander, the Duke of Bourbon, dies in the first assault, shot while climbing a ladder.

What follows is eight months of hell.

Pope Clement the Seventh flees through a secret passage — the Passetto di Borgo — to the fortress of Castel Sant'Angelo. Behind him, the Swiss Guard fights to the last man. One hundred forty-seven of one hundred eighty-nine die buying him time.

The soldiers have no one to restrain them. Cardinals are held for ransom. Nuns are sold in markets. Churches become stables. Relics are mocked, altars stripped, the city systematically plundered. Rome's population drops from fifty-five thousand to ten thousand — through murder, famine, and the plague that finally drives the army south.

The High Renaissance dies here, in fire and pestilence.

But something strange happens in the ashes.

In Florence, while Rome burns, a painter named Jacopo Pontormo is completing an altarpiece. His Deposition from the Cross shows figures that seem to float in grief — no ground beneath them, no cross visible, bodies spiraling in impossible pinks and electric blues. This is Mannerism: art that abandons classical harmony for emotional intensity, elegance pushed until it becomes unease.

And when the artists flee Rome, they carry this new style with them.

Rosso Fiorentino, imprisoned by imperial troops, eventually escapes to France. He brings Mannerism to the court of Francis the First at Fontainebleau, founding the first school of French Renaissance art. Parmigianino flees to Bologna. Perino del Vaga reaches Genoa. Within a decade, the diaspora of Roman artists transforms courts from Paris to Prague.

Destruction becomes transmission.

The same year, Baldassare Castiglione sends his manuscript of The Book of the Courtier to the press. It will appear just before his death — a nostalgic memorial to the elegant court culture being crushed between Habsburg and Valois armies. The Courtier codifies sprezzatura: the art of making difficult things look effortless. It becomes Europe's conduct manual for centuries.

But Castiglione himself, serving as papal ambassador in Spain, learns of Rome's destruction by letter. Pope Clement blames him for failing to prevent it. He dies two years later, heartbroken.

The Sack of fifteen twenty-seven ends an era. The confident humanism that believed education and beauty could reform the world lies in ruins. But from those ruins springs something new: the Counter-Reformation's disciplined piety, the Mannerist style's anxious elegance, the French Renaissance born from Italian exile.

The High Renaissance dies in Rome. But its children conquer Europe.
