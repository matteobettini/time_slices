Eighteen forty-two. Across the Russian provinces, a conman named Chichikov travels from estate to estate, buying something unusual: dead serfs. In Russia's tax system, peasants remained on the rolls until the next census — which meant that owning "dead souls" was profitable on paper. Nikolai Gogol's satire cuts both ways: the literal dead souls of the tax rolls, and the moral emptiness of the landowners who trade them.

In Rome that same year, an American painter named Thomas Cole completes his allegorical masterpiece, The Voyage of Life. Four paintings trace a voyager along the River of Life — from infancy through ambitious youth, through the tempests of manhood, to the peaceful acceptance of death. Cole had founded the Hudson River School, seeing America's wilderness as sacred text. But here in Catholic Rome, he paints Protestant allegory: the youth reaching for a shimmering castle in the sky, which will prove illusion; the old man glimpsing angelic light through parting clouds.

In Paris, Auguste Comte completes the final volume of his Course of Positive Philosophy. This is philosophy as funeral oration. Comte declares that human thought progresses through three stages: theological, metaphysical, and finally positive — scientific. In the positive stage, questions of ultimate causes are abandoned entirely. Only what can be measured counts. Comte coins a new word for this measured study of society: sociology. Meanwhile, also in Paris, Stendhal dies — the author of The Red and the Black, that cold dissection of ambition, passing almost unnoticed. His executor called him "the most improbable of geniuses."

In England, Tennyson publishes his Poems, including "Morte d'Arthur" — the dying king passing Excalibur to Bedivere as the old order dissolves. Robert Browning publishes "My Last Duchess," where the Duke of Ferrara coolly describes a portrait of his murdered wife. Death and dissolution everywhere — in art, in literature, in philosophy.

And in the wider world: the Treaty of Nanking ends the First Opium War, forcing China to cede Hong Kong to Britain. The Chartist general strike erupts across England's industrial Midlands — half a million workers pulling plugs from steam boilers to halt the factories, demanding to be counted equally through universal suffrage.

The theme is enumeration. Who counts? What counts? Gogol's dead souls on tax rolls. Chartists demanding every man be counted. Comte declaring that only the measurable is real. In a world increasingly measured by abstractions — labor-hours, commodity prices, census figures — artists and philosophers struggled to find where the human soul might still reside.
