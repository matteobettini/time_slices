Eighteen ninety-five. The year the world learned to see itself anew.

On the evening of November eighth, in a laboratory at the University of Würzburg, a physicist named Wilhelm Röntgen noticed something strange. A fluorescent screen was glowing across a darkened room — even though the cathode ray tube he was experimenting with was completely covered. Some kind of invisible radiation was passing through the cardboard, through the air, through everything.

By December, Röntgen had photographed his wife Anna Bertha's hand. The image showed her bones, her wedding ring, the skeleton beneath her living flesh. When she saw the photograph, she reportedly said: "I have seen my death."

Six weeks later, on December twenty-eighth, in a basement room beneath the Grand Café on the Boulevard des Capucines in Paris, the Lumière brothers screened moving pictures to a paying audience for the first time. Workers leaving a factory. A train arriving at a station. Reality captured and replayed at will. Legend has it the train film caused panic — audiences believing they'd be struck. The story is probably exaggerated, but it captures the genuine shock of seeing time itself made manipulable.

Meanwhile, in London, Oscar Wilde was at the summit of his career. On Valentine's Day, his masterpiece The Importance of Being Earnest premiered — wit so light it seemed merely decorative, satire so elegant it looked like entertainment. Two plays running simultaneously in the West End. The Aesthete triumphant.

Within weeks, everything collapsed. Wilde sued the Marquess of Queensberry for libel; the trial revealed what Victorian society refused to see. By May, Wilde was convicted of gross indecency and sentenced to two years' hard labor. The Aesthetic movement died with its prophet's imprisonment. What had seemed like innocent art turned out to contain dangerous truths.

Thomas Hardy published Jude the Obscure that same year. The Bishop of Wakefield publicly burned his copy — the novel's frank treatment of marriage and sexuality too much for Victorian propriety to bear. Hardy never wrote another novel.

And H.G. Wells published The Time Machine, inventing the very concept of a vehicle for traveling through time. The fourth dimension made traversable. Past and future as destinations rather than abstractions.

In Venice, on April thirtieth, the first Biennale opened — two hundred and twenty-four thousand visitors coming to see contemporary art displayed as international exhibition. Art institutionalized, made commodity, given a permanent address.

In Paris, Cézanne had his first solo show at Vollard's gallery. The hermit of Aix finally visible, his strange geometric explorations reaching the young artists who would become Cubists.

Everywhere you look in eighteen ninety-five, you see new forms of vision colliding with violent resistance. X-rays see through the body — and Anna Röntgen recoils from her own skeleton. Cinema captures reality — and audiences flee from a train that isn't there. Wilde's wit sees through Victorian hypocrisy — and society destroys him for it. Hardy sees through the marriage plot — and is silenced. The nineteenth century was learning to see itself as others would see it: a skeleton beneath the confident flesh.

These technologies and these scandals weren't connected by influence — Röntgen knew nothing of Wilde's trials, the Lumières nothing of Hardy's novel. What connected them was the historical moment. The positivist confidence of the mid-nineteenth century was cracking. Science could now reveal what eyes couldn't see. Art could now capture what memory couldn't hold. And literature could now say what propriety forbade.

In eighteen ninety-five, new eyes opened everywhere. And what they saw would take a century to comprehend.
