The year seventeen ninety-four. In Paris, the guillotine blade falls so often they must replace it twice a week. In London, a visionary poet asks the most dangerous question: who made the Tyger?

On June eighth, Maximilien Robespierre descends a man-made mountain at the Champ de Mars, dressed in sky-blue coat and tricolor sash, presiding over the Festival of the Supreme Being. Jacques-Louis David has designed everything — the artificial peaks, the massed choirs, the burning of an effigy of Atheism. This is no Christian ceremony. Robespierre has replaced God with Reason wearing a liturgical mask. The Supreme Being is a philosopher's deity, an Enlightenment abstraction made into civic religion. The Revolution will have its own worship, its own priests, its own martyrs.

It will also have its own executions. The Law of twenty-two Prairial, passed just two days later, strips defendants of lawyers and limits verdicts to freedom or death. In the six weeks of the Great Terror that follow, over thirteen hundred people are guillotined in Paris alone.

Meanwhile, in a cramped London workshop, William Blake prints his Songs of Experience — each page illuminated by hand, text and image inseparable. The opening lines of The Tyger have already become the most famous question in English poetry: Tyger Tyger, burning bright, in the forests of the night — what immortal hand or eye could frame thy fearful symmetry?

Blake is asking what Robespierre cannot. If God made both the gentle lamb and the burning predator, what kind of creator is this? Blake's Urizen — his mythological figure of cold reason — appears in Europe, a Prophecy that same year, crouched in a circle of light, compass in hand, measuring the infinite void. Reason as tyrant. The very force the Jacobins worship.

In a Paris prison, the philosopher Condorcet has already finished his final work — the Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind. Even while hiding from the very Revolution he supported, Condorcet writes of humanity's inevitable march toward enlightenment. He will die in March, probably by his own hand, his optimism a monument to a faith the Terror has already disproved.

And André Chénier, the poet who dared criticize the Jacobins, smuggles his verses out of Saint-Lazare prison in his dirty laundry. His Iambes burn with fury: like a flock of bleating sheep, Soissons, Meaux, Versailles... all to the scaffold. He is guillotined on July twenty-fifth — three days before Robespierre himself falls to the blade.

On nine Thermidor — July twenty-seventh — the Convention finally turns on its own chairman. Robespierre is dragged to the same guillotine that has claimed so many. The Terror ends.

But the question Blake asked remains unanswered. What immortal hand or eye framed this fearful symmetry — a revolution that promised liberty and delivered death? The Romantics who come after — Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron — will carry the wound for a generation. Reason promised liberation. Reason delivered Terror.

Who made the Tyger? The same hand that made the lamb.
