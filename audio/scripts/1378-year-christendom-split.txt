The year thirteen seventy-eight. In Rome, a mob surrounds the papal conclave, screaming for an Italian pope. In Florence, wool carders storm the seat of government. In Padua, a painter fills a chapel wall with over a hundred faces, each one watching Christ die.

This is the year Christendom splits in two.

In April, terrified cardinals elect Bartolomeo Prignano as Pope Urban the Sixth. He's Italian — the first in seventy years — but within months, his abrasive personality alienates everyone. He insults cardinals to their faces. He demands reforms that threaten their wealth. By September, the French cardinals have had enough. They meet at Fondi and elect a rival: Robert of Geneva becomes Clement the Seventh.

Two popes. Each excommunicating the other's followers. Europe divides along political lines — France, Scotland, Castile back Clement in Avignon; England, the German states, most of Italy back Urban in Rome. For nearly forty years, Christians will not know who speaks for God.

In Florence, a different revolution is underway. On July twenty-first, the Ciompi — wool carders, dyers, unskilled workers who have no guild, no vote, no voice — seize the Palazzo Vecchio. Michele di Lando, a wool carder, becomes Gonfaloniere of Justice. Three new guilds are created overnight. For six extraordinary weeks, the workers govern Florence.

It doesn't last. By late August, the major guilds have reasserted control. But the question the Ciompi asked echoes through history: if authority comes from the people, why not all the people?

In Padua, Altichiero da Zevio is finishing his masterpiece. The Crucifixion in the Chapel of San Giacomo fills an entire wall with humanity: soldiers casting lots, women weeping, disciples frozen in grief, bystanders who look away. Petrarch's face appears in the crowd — he died four years ago, but Altichiero paints him watching the cross. Each figure responds differently to the same event. There is no single authorized reaction.

And in England, William Langland is revising Piers Plowman — his great dream-vision of a corrupted world where even the Church has lost its way. He writes in Middle English, not Latin. The people's tongue for the people's truth.

Meanwhile, Catherine of Siena — mystic, diplomat, a woman who couldn't write until she was thirty — has finished dictating her Dialogue of Divine Providence. She speaks to God directly, bypassing priests and cardinals. When the schism breaks out, she rushes to Rome to defend Urban, writing letters that call Clement's cardinals "incarnate demons."

Thirteen seventy-eight. The center cannot hold because people have stopped believing it was ever the center. Two popes. A workers' government. A hundred faces at the cross. The old certainties crack — and through the cracks, something new begins to grow.
