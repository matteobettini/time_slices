London is burning.

It's the second of September, sixteen sixty-six. A baker named Thomas Farriner falls asleep without fully dousing his oven on Pudding Lane. By three in the morning, flames are leaping between timber-framed houses packed so close you can reach across and touch your neighbor's wall.

Samuel Pepys, naval administrator and compulsive diarist, is woken by his maid. He looks out the window, decides the fire is "far enough off," and goes back to sleep. Hours later, he watches from the Tower as "one entire arch of fire" consumes the city of Chaucer and Shakespeare. He describes pigeons burning their wings rather than abandon their perches. He buries his Parmesan cheese and wine in the garden. 

For four days, London burns. Old Saint Paul's Cathedral, eight hundred years of English Christianity, melts — its lead roof running down Ludgate Hill like water. Eighty-seven parish churches. Thirteen thousand houses. The medieval city ceases to exist.

But here's what makes sixteen sixty-six miraculous.

Two hundred miles north, in a farmhouse in Lincolnshire, a twenty-three-year-old sits alone. Cambridge University has closed because of plague. Isaac Newton has nothing to do but think.

He passes sunlight through a prism and discovers that white light is composed of colors. He fills notebooks with calculations about rates of change — what we now call calculus. And watching an apple fall in his mother's orchard, he begins reasoning toward a single law that will explain why planets orbit and apples drop.

None of this will be published for twenty years. Newton guards his discoveries like secrets. But the scientific revolution is happening in that quiet farmhouse while London burns.

In Delft, Johannes Vermeer paints a young woman turning toward the viewer, lips parted, an impossibly large pearl catching the light. The Girl with a Pearl Earring. Vermeer uses the camera obscura to study how light actually behaves — the same year Newton splits light into its spectrum.

In Paris, Molière stages The Misanthrope. Alceste rages against a society that cannot tell truth from performance.

John Dryden will call it the "year of miracles." He's being ironic about the disasters. But somehow he's also right. From plague and fire comes a modern city — Christopher Wren will rebuild Saint Paul's as an English Baroque masterpiece. From intellectual exile comes modern physics.

Catastrophe enables transformation. Isolation forces creation. 

Pepys dug up his cheese afterward. It was perfectly preserved.

Sixteen sixty-six. The year of fire and light.
