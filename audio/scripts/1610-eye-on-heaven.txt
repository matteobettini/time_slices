Sixteen ten. A year when certainty dies — and new ways of seeing are born.

In March, a forty-six-year-old professor in Venice rushes a sixty-page book to press. Galileo Galilei has spent the winter pointing a new instrument at the sky — a telescope, refined from Dutch spectacle-makers' designs. What he sees will end two thousand years of cosmic certainty.

The Moon is not smooth. It has mountains, craters, shadows that shift with the Sun. The Milky Way is not a smear of celestial light — it's made of countless individual stars, too faint for the naked eye. And Jupiter — Jupiter has moons. Four of them, orbiting the giant planet like a miniature solar system.

That last discovery is the killing blow. For fifteen hundred years, Aristotelian cosmology has insisted that everything in the heavens orbits Earth. Now Galileo has seen proof that Earth is not the only center of orbital motion. The old model is dead. He names Jupiter's moons the Medicean Stars, securing patronage from the Grand Duke of Tuscany. Science and politics already intertwined.

The book is called Sidereus Nuncius — The Starry Messenger. Within weeks, Johannes Kepler in Prague reads it and writes back with jubilant support. The astronomical community splits. Some refuse to look through telescopes at all, claiming lenses distort truth. But the young and the curious look — and they see what Galileo saw.

In Rome, a painter is watching. Lodovico Cigoli, Galileo's friend, has been commissioned to fresco the dome of the Pauline Chapel in Santa Maria Maggiore. The subject is the Assumption of the Virgin, and tradition demands Mary stand upon a perfect, immaculate moon. But Cigoli has read the Sidereus Nuncius. Under Mary's feet, he paints a moon with craters and mountains — the first depiction in Christian art of Galileo's imperfect cosmos.

Meanwhile, another painter lies dying.

Caravaggio has been a fugitive for four years, ever since he killed a man in Rome in a fight over tennis, or a woman, or a debt — the sources disagree. He has fled to Naples, Malta, Sicily, painting masterpieces while running for his life. His final works are the darkest he ever made. In David with the Head of Goliath, he paints his own face on the severed head — a visual plea for the papal pardon that might let him return to Rome.

On July eighteenth, Caravaggio dies on a beach at Porto Ercole, age thirty-eight. The pardon has been granted, but it arrives too late. The greatest painter of his age expires in obscurity, probably from fever, perhaps from infected wounds, maybe from lead poisoning from his own paints.

But the revolution in seeing that he began continues. His tenebrism — figures emerging from darkness into dramatic light — will shape European painting for a century. And that light? It falls like evidence. Specific, undeniable. The same empirical gaze that Galileo turned on the heavens.

Two months before Caravaggio's death, another kind of certainty is destroyed. On May fourteenth, François Ravaillac, a Catholic fanatic, stabs King Henry the Fourth of France in his carriage on a Paris street. Henry — who converted from Protestantism with the famous words "Paris is worth a Mass" — had ended decades of religious war. His Edict of Nantes guaranteed tolerance for Protestants.

Ravaillac believes Henry is about to make war on the Pope. He is wrong, but certainty does not require truth. The king dies instantly. France passes to his nine-year-old son, Louis the Thirteenth, under a regency. The fragile peace will last another generation before the continent explodes into the Thirty Years' War.

In London, John Donne is writing his Holy Sonnets. "The new philosophy calls all in doubt," he will soon write. "The element of fire is quite put out; the Sun is lost, and the Earth, and no man's wit can well direct him where to look for it."

Doubt. That is what sixteen ten has given us. Doubt about the heavens, doubt about religious authority, doubt about whether our eyes tell us truth or the ancients do.

But Galileo has an answer. Look through the lens. See for yourself. The scientific revolution is not yet a program. It is, for now, just an eye — newly opened, that cannot look away.
