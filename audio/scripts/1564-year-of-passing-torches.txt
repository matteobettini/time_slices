Fifteen sixty-four. The Year of Passing Torches.

On February eighteenth, Michelangelo dies in Rome. He is eighty-eight years old. Six days earlier, his chisel was still striking marble — the Pietà Rondanini, a sculpture he would never finish. Where his first Pietà showed grief polished to perfection, this final work strips everything away. Christ's body dissolves into his mother's embrace. The Renaissance, exhausted, reaches for something beyond itself.

Three days before Michelangelo's death, in Pisa, a boy is born named Galileo Galilei.

Two months later, on April twenty-sixth, in Stratford-upon-Avon, the parish register records: William, son of John Shakespeare, baptized. And sixty days before that, in Canterbury, Christopher Marlowe was baptized — the poet who will write Doctor Faustus before dying in a tavern brawl at twenty-nine.

The two greatest dramatists in English arrive within weeks of each other. The man who will revolutionize astronomy arrives three days before the greatest artist of the Renaissance departs.

In May, John Calvin dies in Geneva. For twenty-five years he had transformed a city into a laboratory of Reformed Christianity. His Institutes of the Christian Religion — predestination, divine sovereignty, the total depravity of man — would shape Protestant thought for centuries. The Reformation loses its architect.

The Catholic Church responds. Within months, Pope Pius IV promulgates the Index of Forbidden Books, the Tridentine Index, with ten rules for censorship that will govern Catholic intellectual life until nineteen sixty-six.

In October, Andreas Vesalius dies on a Greek island, returning from Jerusalem. Twenty-one years earlier, his Fabric of the Human Body had overturned Galen — proved that ancient authorities could be wrong, that observation trumps tradition. The founder of modern anatomy follows the founder of Reformed theology to the grave.

The coincidence is less coincidence than hinge. Michelangelo abandons perfection. Calvin's systematic theology is complete. Vesalius's anatomical method awaits new applications. Into this space, the newborns arrive: Galileo, who will turn observation on the heavens. Shakespeare and Marlowe, who will turn questions of fate and free will into theater.

Three days separate Galileo's birth from Michelangelo's death — as if creation paused to hand off its tools.

Fifteen sixty-four — when the Renaissance handed its unfinished work to the age of observation.
