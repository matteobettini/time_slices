Seventeen fourteen. The Year of Locks.

In Hanover, an aging philosopher completes his final metaphysical system. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz writes the Monadology — ninety paragraphs that describe a universe stranger than any fiction. Reality, he argues, consists of monads: simple, indivisible substances, each one "windowless." Nothing enters; nothing leaves. Every monad reflects the entire universe from its own unique perspective, but no monad ever touches another.

How, then, does the world seem connected? Pre-established harmony. At the moment of creation, God designed every substance to unfold in perfect synchronisation — like clocks set together, ticking forever in agreement without ever communicating.

Leibniz serves George Ludwig, Elector of Hanover. But when Queen Anne dies on the first of August — after seventeen pregnancies and no surviving heir — George inherits the crown of Great Britain. He becomes King George the First. And he forbids Leibniz from following.

The philosopher of harmony dies alone in seventeen sixteen, attended only by his secretary.

Meanwhile, in London, Alexander Pope publishes the definitive version of The Rape of the Lock — a mock-epic about a stolen curl of hair. Lord Petre had snipped a lock from Arabella Fermor's head; Pope transforms this drawing-room scandal into a Homeric battlefield, complete with guardian sylphs and celestial machinery. His couplets gleam like cut diamonds: "What mighty contests rise from trivial things."

Pope is twenty-six and already England's greatest living poet. The six engravings that illustrate the poem bring satire visual wit.

And in Paris, Antoine Watteau is inventing something new. His paintings — elegant figures in twilight gardens, masked revellers from the Commedia dell'arte, lovers in suspended conversation — create a genre the Académie will call the fête galante. But look closer. These pleasure-seekers never quite meet each other's eyes. They gather in the same enchanted space yet remain lost in private dreams. Watteau paints connection and isolation simultaneously. He will die of tuberculosis at thirty-six. His art already knows.

Bernard Mandeville publishes The Fable of the Bees, arguing that private vices create public benefits — each selfish actor unknowingly serves the hive. This early economic thought prefigures Adam Smith's invisible hand.

The year's recurring image: everything connected, nothing touching. Leibniz's windowless monads. Pope's invisible sylphs. Watteau's revellers who never quite meet. Mandeville's bees who build together without knowing. Even the Hanoverian succession follows an impersonal mechanism — the Act of Settlement — that makes a German prince king of England without meeting his subjects.

Seventeen fourteen. The Enlightenment reveals a clockwork universe — perfectly harmonised, eternally separate. And somewhere in Hanover, a philosopher who believed God had chosen the best of all possible worlds waits for an invitation that never comes.
