Nineteen thirty-four. The year the twentieth century showed its true face.

On the night of June thirtieth, Adolf Hitler orders the murder of his own movement. The SA brownshirts who helped him seize power are now inconvenient — their leader Ernst Röhm wants a second revolution, and the army wants him gone. In three days, at least eighty-five people die: shot in prison cells, gunned down in their homes, killed without trial or appeal. Hitler calls it necessary surgery. When President Hindenburg dies five weeks later, Hitler merges both offices into one: Führer. The German army swears personal loyalty not to Germany, but to him.

In Moscow, Stalin's bureaucrats complete another kind of purge — not of bodies, but of art. The First Congress of Soviet Writers adopts Socialist Realism as mandatory doctrine. Art must serve the people, which means it must serve the Party. The avant-garde experiments that once made Russian art revolutionary are now counter-revolutionary. Optimism is required. Ambiguity is forbidden.

In Hollywood, a different kind of control takes hold. From July first, the Hays Code is strictly enforced. No more showing married couples in the same bed. No more moral ambiguity. The wildness of Pre-Code cinema — its frankness about sex, its complicated criminals — ends overnight.

Yet freedom finds its edges.

In Paris, in September, a small press publishes what American customs immediately bans: Henry Miller's Tropic of Cancer. It's barely a novel — more like an ecstatic howl, autobiography dissolving into philosophy into pornography into prayer. Miller writes as if the boundary between life and literature doesn't exist. Because for him, it doesn't.

In the same city, Max Ernst assembles one hundred eighty-two collages in three weeks. He takes the respectable engravings of Victorian illustrated novels and slices them into nightmares: men with bird heads, women with lion bodies, floods and earthquakes rendered with surgical precision. The images look almost normal at first — and then they don't. Surrealism's masterpiece of unease.

And in Vienna, a young philosopher named Karl Popper publishes a book that will reshape how we understand science itself. His argument: science doesn't advance by proving things true. It advances by proving things false. A theory is scientific only if you can specify what would disprove it. Otherwise it's just ideology — unfalsifiable, and therefore meaningless.

The same year. Three systems of control crystallizing: fascist, communist, commercial. And three acts of radical freedom: Miller's prose, Ernst's scissors, Popper's logic.

Nineteen thirty-four asks the question the century will keep asking: when everything closes in, what remains possible?
