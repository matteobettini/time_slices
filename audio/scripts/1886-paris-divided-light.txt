Eighteen eighty-six. A year when everything splits in two.

On May fifteenth, the eighth and final Impressionist exhibition opens in Paris. And there, in the center of everything, hangs a canvas ten feet wide that will bury Impressionism even as it celebrates the movement. Georges Seurat's "A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte" — two years of meticulous labor, composed entirely of tiny dots of pure color. Stand close and you see only chaos. Step back and Parisians materialize: ladies with parasols, gentlemen in top hats, a monkey on a leash, Sunday afternoon assembling itself from fragments.

Critic Félix Fénéon calls it Neo-Impressionism. Where Monet captured fleeting impressions, Seurat constructs them scientifically. He has turned perception itself into a mechanism.

That same March, Vincent van Gogh arrives in Paris from the coal country of Antwerp. The somber Dutch palette that gave us "The Potato Eaters" is about to shatter against French light. He discovers Japanese prints, meets Toulouse-Lautrec, experiments with those maddening little dots. In two years, his colors will explode. But first, he must unlearn everything.

In January, on the other side of the Channel, Robert Louis Stevenson publishes "The Strange Case of Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde." Forty thousand copies sell in six months. The twist — Jekyll is Hyde — shocks Victorian England. But the deeper message haunts them: respectability itself is the mask. Every gentleman contains his own monster.

And in his lonely Swiss boarding house, Friedrich Nietzsche publishes "Beyond Good and Evil" at his own expense, after publishers refuse it. The title is the program: transcend the slave morality that splits the world into good and evil. Morality is not universal truth — it is perspective, genealogy, will to power. He calls for new philosophers who will be "commanders and law-givers." Three years from now he will collapse in Turin, his productive life ended. But this year, he is razor-sharp.

On May fifteenth — the same day the Impressionist exhibition opens — Emily Dickinson dies in Amherst, Massachusetts. Her sister Lavinia discovers eighteen hundred poems in a locked chest. A secret doubled life: the visible recluse was an invisible revolutionary.

And history itself divides. On May fourth, a bomb explodes at Chicago's Haymarket Square during a labor rally for the eight-hour day. Eight anarchist leaders are convicted despite no evidence linking them to the bomber. Haymarket becomes labor's martyrology, the origin of May Day. Five months later, President Cleveland dedicates the Statue of Liberty: "Liberty Enlightening the World." Freedom proclaimed while labor is crushed. The split is perfect.

This is the year's theme: division revealing depth. Seurat divides light into dots. Stevenson divides the self into masks. Nietzsche divides morality and calls for transcendence. The view from close and the view from far are utterly different. Both are true.

Eighteen eighty-six. The year the surface cracked — and showed us what was underneath.
