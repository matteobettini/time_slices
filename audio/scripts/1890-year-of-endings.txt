In eighteen ninety, the nineteenth century looks in the mirror and sees its own mortality.

Auvers-sur-Oise, a village north of Paris. Vincent van Gogh has been here seventy days. He paints with a fury that frightens the locals — one canvas per day, sometimes two. Wheatfields under turbulent skies. Churches that seem to twist. Dr. Gachet's garden, the town hall, the rooms where he sleeps.

On July twenty-seventh, he walks into a field with a revolver. Two days later, his brother Theo at his bedside, he dies at thirty-seven. He has sold perhaps two paintings in his life. Almost no one knows what has been lost.

In Cambridge, Massachusetts, William James publishes The Principles of Psychology — fourteen hundred pages, twelve years in the making. He coins a phrase that will reshape how we understand the mind: stream of consciousness. Thought, he argues, is not a chain of distinct ideas linked together like beads on a string. It's a river. It flows. No moment is ever quite the same as the last.

In Norway, a young writer named Knut Hamsun reads this and recognizes his own technique. His novel Hunger, published the same year, follows a starving writer through Christiania — not his actions, but his thoughts. Irrational, looping, desperate. The first stream-of-consciousness novel, written the same year the term was invented.

In Amherst, Massachusetts, four years after Emily Dickinson's death, her sister Lavinia publishes what she found in a locked chest: eighteen hundred poems, written in secret, shared with almost no one. The first collection appears in November — heavily edited to smooth Dickinson's wild punctuation, her slant rhymes, her dashes that hang in the air like held breath. The editors think they're cleaning her up. They're actually muffling a revolution. Eleven printings in two years.

And in South Dakota, on December twenty-ninth, the Seventh Cavalry surrounds three hundred Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek. They have come to suppress the Ghost Dance — a movement promising that proper ritual will reunite the living with the dead and end the white man's expansion westward.

A shot is fired. Then the Hotchkiss guns open up.

Between two hundred fifty and three hundred Lakota die that morning. Most are women, children, elders. Most are unarmed. Twenty soldiers receive the Medal of Honor.

The Census Bureau announces that same year that a continuous line of frontier no longer exists. The American West is, officially, settled. The Ghost Dance's promise of restoration ends in massacre.

Three paths leading nowhere. A river that never repeats. A locked chest finally opened. A frontier that closes.

The nineteenth century confronts what comes next.
