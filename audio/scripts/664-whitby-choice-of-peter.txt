Six sixty-four. A windswept monastery on a cliff above the North Sea. A king must choose between two ways of counting time — and in doing so, choose the future of English Christianity.

The problem sounds absurd to modern ears: when does Easter fall? But in the seventh century, this question splits households. King Oswiu of Northumbria follows Celtic custom. His queen, Eanfled, follows Roman. Some years, the king celebrates the holiest feast while his wife is still fasting for Lent. The royal household cannot even break bread together.

Oswiu summons a council to settle the matter. The setting is extraordinary: Streonæshalch, a double monastery ruled by Abbess Hilda — a woman whose authority rivals that of any bishop. Under her governance, both monks and nuns live, study, and pray. Five future bishops will train within these walls.

Colman, Bishop of Lindisfarne, speaks for Celtic tradition. Our way descends from Columba, he argues, and ultimately from John the Evangelist himself. It has served us for generations.

Wilfrid speaks for Rome. Peter, he declares, received the keys of heaven from Christ himself. Rome preserves Peter's practice. Can you, he challenges Colman, claim for your Columba what Christ promised to Peter?

The silence stretches.

King Oswiu smiles. I will not risk offending the keeper of heaven's gate. England chooses Rome.

Colman cannot accept the verdict. He withdraws to Iona, then to Ireland, carrying relics of Saint Aidan. An entire tradition departs with him.

But what remains is just as remarkable. That same year, in that same monastery, a cowherd named Caedmon receives — according to Bede — the miraculous gift of verse. Commanded in a dream to sing of Creation, the illiterate herdsman wakes with a hymn he has never heard before. Nine alliterative lines, praising God as the Guardian of the kingdom of heaven. The first poem in English.

And in the scriptoria of Ireland and Northumbria, monks are creating something equally new: the Book of Durrow, whose carpet pages weave Celtic spirals, Germanic interlace, and Mediterranean colour into devotional geometry. Neither Roman nor Celtic alone — a fusion born of both.

This is what Whitby teaches us. Choices that seem like endings can be beginnings. Colman departs; Caedmon sings. One tradition withdraws; another is born.

Six sixty-four. The year England chose Peter — and poetry chose English.
