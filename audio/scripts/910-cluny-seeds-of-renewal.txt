Nine ten. A year when the old world crumbled — and the new one put down its first roots.

On September eleventh, in a forest clearing in Burgundy, a duke named William gave away his favorite hunting ground. He was called William the Pious, and the land he surrendered would become the Abbey of Cluny — a name that would echo through three centuries of European history.

The gift came with conditions that seemed modest but proved revolutionary. The new monastery would answer directly to the Pope, bypassing every local lord and bishop. The monks would elect their own abbot. No king, no count, no ecclesiastical authority could interfere. It was an island of spiritual independence in a sea of feudal chaos.

And chaos it was. Just weeks before, at the Battle of Tettenhall in August, the combined armies of Wessex and Mercia had finally broken the Viking threat. Three Norse kings lay dead on the field — Eowils, Halfdan, and Ingwær. For nearly a century, Vikings had terrorized England. Now Edward the Elder and his sister Æthelflæd could begin reconquering the Danelaw. The House of Wessex would unite England within a generation.

But across the Channel, darkness was falling. That summer, Hungarian horsemen had swept through East Francia like a plague of locusts. At the Battle of Augsburg in June, they shattered the Frankish army. At Rednitz, ten days later, they did it again. The young king, Louis — called the Child because he had taken the throne at six — was forced to pay tribute. He would be dead within a year, the last Carolingian ruler in the East.

This was the world that Cluny was born into. The great empire of Charlemagne had fractured into squabbling kingdoms. The Carolingian renaissance — the revival of learning, the illuminated manuscripts, the dream of a Christian Roman Empire — seemed to be dying.

And yet. In monasteries across northern France, scribes continued their work. The Franco-Saxon style of illumination — Carolingian elegance blended with the intricate interlace of the British Isles — survived in places like Saint-Amand and Saint-Bertin. They weren't creating masterpieces anymore. They were preserving them. Copying the Rule of Saint Benedict. Maintaining libraries. Keeping the flame alive.

Cluny's independence from local politics meant it could accumulate wealth and prestige without the constant threat of plunder or interference. Within two centuries, over fifteen hundred monasteries would follow its reforms. Four Cluniac monks would become popes. The seed planted in that Burgundian forest would flower into the institutional Church of the High Middle Ages.

The ninth of September, nine ten. Vikings dying at Tettenhall. Hungarians devastating the Frankish heartland. A young king paying tribute to steppe warriors. And in a clearing in Burgundy, a duke signing away a hunting forest — planting, without knowing it, the seeds of renewal.
