Fifteen twenty. The Year of Ruptures.

On Good Friday, April sixth, Raphael dies in Rome. He is thirty-seven years old. At the head of his bier stands his final masterpiece — The Transfiguration — still unfinished. The upper half shows Christ in divine glory on Mount Tabor. The lower half shows the apostles failing to heal a possessed boy. Transcendence and human weakness on one canvas, unresolved. His student Giulio Romano will complete it. But contemporaries understood: the High Renaissance had painted its last harmony.

That same spring, Martin Luther is writing. Three treatises in four months, each pushing further from Rome.

In August, Address to the Christian Nobility of the German Nation appears — written in German, not Latin, selling four thousand copies in three weeks. Luther calls on secular rulers to reform the Church. In October, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church attacks the entire sacramental system. And in November, On the Freedom of a Christian offers a paradox: a Christian is perfectly free, subject to none; a Christian is perfectly dutiful, servant to all. Freedom through surrender. Grace, not works.

On December tenth, Luther makes his choice visible. At the Elster Gate in Wittenberg, before professors and students, he burns the papal bull Exsurge Domine — the document threatening his excommunication. Into the flames go volumes of canon law. The medieval synthesis of faith and reason, already cracked, now breaks open.

Meanwhile, the great powers stage a pantomime of unity. In June, Henry the Eighth and Francis the First meet at the Field of Cloth of Gold — eighteen days of tournaments and feasts on golden fabric. A fantasy of chivalric brotherhood. It changes nothing. The rivalry between England, France, and Habsburg Spain will shape the century.

In September, Suleiman the Magnificent becomes Ottoman sultan. His forty-six year reign will threaten Vienna and dominate the Mediterranean.

And across the Atlantic, on the night of June thirtieth — La Noche Triste. The Aztecs drive Cortés from Tenochtitlan. Hundreds of Spanish soldiers drown in the causeways, weighed down by stolen gold. The conquest will continue. But the empire of Moctezuma has shown it can fight back.

Albrecht Dürer travels through the Netherlands that year. He attends Charles the Fifth's coronation in Aachen. He views Aztec treasures sent by Cortés and writes in his diary: I have seen nothing in all my days that so delighted my heart as these things.

Old worlds end. New ones are not yet born. Fifteen twenty — the year of ruptures.
