Eighteen sixteen. The Year Without a Summer.

In April of eighteen fifteen, Mount Tambora in Indonesia explodes — the largest volcanic eruption in recorded human history. One hundred fifty cubic kilometres of ash and sulphur blast into the stratosphere. The world doesn't know it yet, but the climate is about to change.

By the summer of eighteen sixteen, the veil of volcanic aerosols has spread across the Northern Hemisphere. Global temperatures drop. In New England, frost kills crops in June. Snow falls in New York in July. Across Europe, the harvest fails catastrophically. Famine stalks Germany, Switzerland, Ireland. Food riots erupt. The skies burn red at sunset — not poetically, but literally.

And at a rain-soaked villa on Lake Geneva, something extraordinary happens.

Lord Byron, the scandalous poet in self-imposed exile, has rented Villa Diodati. Percy Bysshe Shelley arrives with his eighteen-year-old lover Mary Godwin and her stepsister Claire. Byron's young physician, John Polidori, joins them. They're trapped indoors by cold, unrelenting rain — in June, in Switzerland.

Byron proposes a ghost story contest. Mary Godwin, sleepless and imaginative, conceives a tale of a man who builds a creature from dead matter and animates it with lightning. She will call it Frankenstein. Polidori sketches what will become The Vampyre — the first modern vampire fiction. And Byron himself writes Darkness — a blank-verse apocalypse: "The bright sun was extinguished, and the stars did wander darkling in the eternal space."

He isn't exaggerating. He's describing what he sees outside his window.

Meanwhile, Coleridge finally publishes Kubla Khan, the opium dream he transcribed nineteen years earlier. Its sunless sea and caverns measureless to man now feel less like fantasy and more like prophecy.

The volcanic darkness creates the conditions for Romantic darkness. Turner begins sketching the fiery sunsets that will define his later career. Shelley writes his Hymn to Intellectual Beauty, searching for meaning in a cosmos that suddenly looks indifferent to human flourishing.

Eighteen sixteen teaches what the philosophers couldn't prove: that nature can turn terrifying, that Providence might just be accident, that the sublime isn't only beautiful — it can overwhelm and destroy.

Gothic horror, apocalyptic poetry, melancholy landscapes. The Romantic turn toward darkness is partly meteorological. A volcano in Indonesia, and teenagers at a Swiss villa, give us nightmares that still haunt us.
