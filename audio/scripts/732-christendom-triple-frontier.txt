Seven thirty-two. Christendom is fighting on three frontiers at once — and somehow, against all odds, it is winning.

On the plains between Tours and Poitiers, Charles Martel draws up his Frankish infantry in a heavy phalanx. For seven days, the Umayyad cavalry probes for weakness. Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi commands the largest Muslim army ever to penetrate this deep into Western Europe. Behind them lies Al-Andalus; beyond, the riches of Gaul and the crumbling Merovingian kingdoms.

On the seventh day, they charge. The Franks stand like a wall of ice, as one chronicler puts it — immovable, fatal to any who approach. When the battle ends, Abd al-Rahman is dead. His army retreats south and will never return.

But Christendom's enemies are not only external. In Constantinople, the most powerful Christian emperor alive has turned on the faith's own images. Leo the Third orders icons stripped from churches and destroyed. For the Emperor, images have become idols — obstacles between the soul and God.

Yet in a monastery called Mar Saba, perched above the Judean wilderness in Muslim-controlled Palestine, a theologian named John of Damascus writes the response that will shape Orthodox and Catholic thought forever. He argues: because God became visible flesh in Christ, matter itself can convey the sacred. The icons are not idols — they are windows. John writes beyond the Emperor's reach. The irony is exquisite: a Christian theologian, safe under Islamic rule, defends Christian images against a Christian emperor who would destroy them.

And on a wind-scoured island off the Northumbrian coast, at the very edge of the known world, a monk named Bede has just completed his masterwork. The Ecclesiastical History of the English People. It is the first comprehensive history of England — and one of the finest historical works the Middle Ages will produce.

Bede does something revolutionary. He dates events using the Anno Domini system — years from Christ's birth — and this modest choice will shape how the entire Western world tells time. He cites his sources. He distinguishes eyewitness testimony from hearsay and legend. He writes history as we would recognize it.

And surrounding Bede's scriptorium are the products of his world's artistic genius: the Lindisfarne Gospels, created just a decade or two earlier by Bishop Eadfrith. Carpet pages that dissolve representation into infinite interlace. Celtic spirals, Germanic animal forms, Mediterranean gold — all fused into something that exceeds its sources.

This is Christendom's eighth-century triple frontier. Charles defends the military border. John defends the theological border. Bede defends the intellectual border — proving that the converted barbarians of Britain can produce scholarship rivalling Rome's golden age.

When Viking raiders destroy Lindisfarne in seven ninety-three, the achievement of this generation will already have seeded something greater. Alcuin of York, trained in Bede's Northumbria, will carry this learning to the court of Charlemagne. The Carolingian Renaissance will rise from these roots.

Seven thirty-two. The year the hammer fell at Tours — and learning shone from the edge of the world.
