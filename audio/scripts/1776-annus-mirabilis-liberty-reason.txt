Seventeen seventy-six. In a single astonishing year, the modern world is born — not once, but four times over.

In Edinburgh, David Hume is dying. The great skeptic, the philosopher who questioned causation itself, who doubted that we could ever know the external world, faces death with such cheerful composure that it scandalizes all of Christian Europe. His friend Adam Smith visits him on his deathbed. Hume jokes about Charon, the ferryman of the dead. He dies on August twenty-fifth, serene and unrepentant. Smith will later publish an account of this peaceful death — and receive more hate mail for it than for anything in his economic writings. How dare a man die well without God?

But Smith has other concerns. On March ninth, he publishes a book seventeen years in the making — An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. Division of labor. Free markets. The invisible hand guiding self-interest toward public good. The vocabulary of capitalism is born. Smith doesn't invent economics — but he transforms it from scattered observations into a systematic science.

Meanwhile in London, another book appears. Edward Gibbon releases the first volume of his Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire — a six-volume autopsy of civilizational collapse that will take twelve more years to complete. His controversial thesis — that Christianity contributed to Rome's fall — outrages the pious. But his method matters more than his conclusions: apply skeptical inquiry to historical sources. Treat claims as evidence to be weighed, not truths to be accepted.

Both Smith and Gibbon share something: the Humean method. Observation over dogma. Systematic analysis over received tradition. The Scottish Enlightenment, radiating from Edinburgh to reshape the world.

And across the Atlantic, on July fourth, that world reshapes itself. The Second Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence. Thomas Jefferson's prose crystallizes Enlightenment philosophy into political action: we hold these truths to be self-evident. The phrase echoes Thomas Reid's Common Sense philosophy — and Thomas Paine's pamphlet of the same name, which has sold half a million copies and made independence thinkable.

The outcome is far from certain. Washington's army is ragged, Britain the greatest military power on Earth. But something has shifted. If God won't guarantee order, if tradition won't justify power, if reason alone must guide human affairs — then humans must create their own foundations.

Hume dies. Smith publishes. Gibbon begins his autopsy of empire. Jefferson declares self-evident truths. Seventeen seventy-six: the year philosophy became economics, history became science, and reason became revolution.
