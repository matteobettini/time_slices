The year 1883 is a year of endings and beginnings. Everything seems to be dying. Everything seems to be starting again.

On March 14th, Karl Marx dies in London, stateless and nearly penniless, outlived by only two of his seven children. Eleven mourners attend the funeral at Highgate Cemetery three days later. Friedrich Engels delivers the eulogy, declaring that Marx discovered the law of human history as Darwin discovered the law of organic nature. The claim is grandiose, but within thirty-five years, Marx's ideas will inspire the Russian Revolution. Within seventy, half the world will live under governments claiming his authority.

The same month Marx dies, Friedrich Nietzsche writes the first part of Thus Spoke Zarathustra in ten days, in a creative explosion at Rapallo on the Italian coast. Zarathustra descends from his mountain to announce that God is dead and the Übermensch must be born. Marx promised collective liberation through historical process. Nietzsche promises individual transformation through will. The two philosophies will haunt the twentieth century, twisted by followers neither man would recognize.

In art, the same pattern: death clearing space for transformation.

On April 20th, Claude Monet moves into a rented house in Giverny, a village on the Seine sixty kilometers northwest of Paris. The famous water garden doesn't exist yet — that comes later — but the light is already perfect. Monet is forty-two, established but restless, Impressionism's most dedicated practitioner still chasing the impossible: to paint not things but the light that touches them.

Eleven days later, Édouard Manet dies in Paris at fifty-one, his left leg amputated from locomotor ataxia caused by syphilis. Manet was never quite an Impressionist — he always wanted Salon acceptance, always worked in his studio — but he made them possible. Olympia, Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe: these scandals of the 1860s cracked open the door through which Monet and the others walked. The teacher dies; the students scatter to their gardens.

Richard Wagner died in Venice in February. In October, the Metropolitan Opera opens in New York with Gounod's Faust — not because the city needed another opera house, but because new money was excluded from old money's boxes. Culture as class warfare, even in the Gilded Age.

Two children's books appear this year that will outlast most serious literature of the decade. Carlo Collodi's Pinocchio completes its Italian serialization and appears in book form — a puppet who wants to be a real boy, darker and more moralistic than later adaptations suggest. And Robert Louis Stevenson's Treasure Island invents modern pirate mythology: the treasure map marked with X, the one-legged sailor with a parrot, the ambiguous villain you can't help admiring.

On May 24th, the Brooklyn Bridge opens after fourteen years of construction — the longest suspension bridge in the world, connecting Manhattan and Brooklyn. President Chester Arthur walks across; 150,000 people follow on opening day. The bridge is engineering as monument, Gothic towers with steel cables, industrial confidence reaching toward cathedral ambition.

And in August, Krakatoa explodes.

The eruption on August 26-27 is among history's largest, audible three thousand miles away in Australia, generating tsunamis that kill over 36,000 people. But the long-term effect is stranger: volcanic ash circles the globe, scattered into the stratosphere, and for years afterward, sunsets burn red worldwide. Global temperatures drop. The sky itself changes color.

Some scholars believe Edvard Munch's blood-red sky in The Scream remembers Krakatoa's atmospheric aftermath — nature itself making art possible.

The year's theme is succession. Endings that are also beginnings. Deaths that clear space for what comes next. Marx dies and Nietzsche rises. Manet dies and Monet gardens. The island destroys itself to birth new sunsets. The bridge that killed twenty-seven workers becomes infrastructure that outlasts everything else built that year.

Some endings open onto futures the dying cannot imagine.
