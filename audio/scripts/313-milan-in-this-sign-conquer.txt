On the twenty-eighth of October, three twelve, the fate of an empire hangs over a bridge.

Constantine marches on Rome with forty thousand men. His rival Maxentius waits behind the city walls with one hundred thousand. The odds are impossible. But the night before battle, Constantine sees something in the sky — a cross of light, and words in Greek: In this sign, conquer.

He orders his soldiers to paint a new symbol on their shields. The Chi-Rho. The first two letters of Christ in Greek.

The next day, Maxentius inexplicably abandons his walls and meets Constantine at the Milvian Bridge. His troops panic. The bridge collapses. Maxentius drowns in the Tiber. Constantine enters Rome as master of the West.

Three months later, in February three thirteen, Constantine meets his eastern counterpart Licinius in Milan. What they agree to will change history forever.

The Edict of Milan grants freedom of religion throughout the Roman Empire. After three centuries of sporadic persecution — after Nero burned Christians as human torches, after Diocletian's Great Persecution filled the arenas with martyrs — the faith is suddenly legal.

The architects rush to celebrate. Near the Colosseum, they begin constructing the largest triumphal arch Rome has ever seen. But there's no time to carve new sculptures, so they do something remarkable: they strip reliefs from monuments of Trajan, Hadrian, Marcus Aurelius. They recarve the imperial heads to look like Constantine. The Arch becomes a palimpsest — a monument built from the cannibalised glory of the past.

Meanwhile, two writers race to compose the new history. Lactantius, the Christian Cicero, writes how every emperor who persecuted Christians met gruesome death. Eusebius of Caesarea begins his Ecclesiastical History — the founding text of church historiography. Both construct the same narrative: Providence wins. Persecution ends. The emperor serves God.

In the Basilica Nova, a colossal statue of Constantine gazes toward heaven with enormous eyes. The head alone stands two and a half metres tall. Classical naturalism is dying. A new art is being born — hieratic, frontal, otherworldly.

But here's the paradox the arch inscription captures perfectly. It credits Constantine's victory to divine instinct — without naming which god. Was this a Christian conversion, or political calculation? Constantine will rule as a Christian emperor for twenty-four years. He will spend more on churches than on the entire military budget.

But he won't be baptised until his deathbed in three thirty-seven. For a quarter century, the most Christian emperor in history remains technically a catechumen — an unbaptised believer.

The Constantinian revolution is messier than Eusebius wants it to be. An arch built from stolen sculptures. An emperor who hesitates at baptism. A vision no one can verify.

But the transformation is real. In one generation, Christianity goes from persecuted sect to favoured faith. The template for church and state — for Christendom itself — is set in motion.

In this sign, conquer. The question of what that sign meant would occupy the next seventeen centuries.
