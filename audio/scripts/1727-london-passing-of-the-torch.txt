March twenty-eighth, seventeen twenty-seven. In Westminster Abbey, pallbearers include the Lord Chancellor and two dukes. The body they carry belongs not to royalty, but to a mathematician.

Isaac Newton is buried with honors no scientist had ever received. The great architect of the universe has finally joined his calculations. In the crowd stands a young French exile named Voltaire, and he is taking notes. He will later write that Newton was buried "like a king who had done well by his subjects." The lesson is clear: in England, a philosopher receives the honors of a prince.

Eight days earlier, Newton had died at eighty-four. He was the last of the great system-builders — men who believed they could decode the cosmos through pure mathematics. His Principia had explained gravity; his Opticks had decomposed light. Now his body lies in the nave, and Voltaire is already composing the narrative that will define Newton for posterity. He even learns the apple legend from Newton's niece. A piece of fruit will explain gravity to schoolchildren for three hundred years.

In June, King George the First dies on the road to Hanover. His son becomes George the Second, and in October, Handel's coronation anthems thunder through Westminster Abbey. "Zadok the Priest" — with its patient instrumental build and explosive choral entrance — will sound at every British coronation thereafter. The German-born composer, newly naturalized, becomes the voice of Hanoverian legitimacy.

Meanwhile, across the Channel, Canaletto is painting Venice in a new way. The Stonemason's Yard shows not gondolas and palaces but workmen chipping stone, laundry hanging between buildings, a child who has just fallen. It is the city as lived, not as toured. This is intimate vedutismo — not the Grand Tour souvenir but daily life rendered with the precision of Dutch genre painting.

In London, James Thomson publishes "Summer," the second installment of The Seasons — blank verse that treats nature as subject rather than backdrop. Thomson has read Newton and Locke; his poetry carries their empirical spirit into verse. And the Reverend Stephen Hales publishes Vegetable Staticks, measuring sap pressure with Newtonian rigor. The quantitative method spreads from stars to stems.

An age of observation is beginning. The torch has passed.
