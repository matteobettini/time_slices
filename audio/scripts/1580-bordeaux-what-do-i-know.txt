On March first, 1580, a middle-aged French nobleman signs the preface to a book unlike any written before. "I am myself the matter of my book," Michel de Montaigne declares. "You would be unreasonable to suspend your leisure on so frivolous and vain a subject."

He is lying, of course — in the way only a genius can lie. The Essays are anything but frivolous. In three volumes of wandering, contradictory, deeply personal prose, Montaigne invents a new form of thinking: the essay, from the French essayer, "to attempt." Not a treatise proving a point. Not a dialogue staging a philosophical debate. Just a man thinking on paper, following his thoughts where they lead, admitting when they contradict each other.

His motto, inscribed on a medal he wore, was "Que sçay-je?" — "What do I know?" The question is not rhetorical. Montaigne genuinely doubts whether human beings can know anything with certainty. We cannot prove we're superior to animals. We cannot trust our senses. We cannot even be sure our reason functions consistently from moment to moment.

This radical skepticism emerges from a specific catastrophe. The French Wars of Religion have been raging for nearly twenty years. Catholics massacre Protestants at Saint Bartholomew's Day in 1572. Protestants besiege Catholic cities. Men who claim absolute certainty about God's will are butchering each other in His name.

Montaigne's response is to doubt everything — except the value of human fellowship and honest self-examination. He retreats to a tower in his family château near Bordeaux, lines the walls with Greek and Latin quotations, and writes. Forty-eight oak beams above his head are painted with ancient wisdom: maxims from Sextus Empiricus, Ecclesiastes, Lucretius.

In this same year, the architect Andrea Palladio dies in August, leaving behind not just buildings but a system. His Four Books of Architecture has taught Europe that classical proportion expresses cosmic order. The Palladian villa — symmetrical, serene, rationally beautiful — will shape everything from English country houses to American democracy's physical form.

Meanwhile in Toledo, Spain, a Greek painter is finishing works that seem to violate every Palladian rule. El Greco stretches his figures toward heaven, bathes them in unearthly light, makes the spiritual visible through distortion. His Disrobing of Christ scandalizes traditionalists but captivates mystics.

Palladio's classical order and El Greco's ecstatic elongation represent opposite responses to the same crisis of meaning. When Copernicus removed Earth from the center of the cosmos, when religious wars shattered the unity of Christendom, how does one rebuild a stable world? Through geometry and proportion? Or through reaching toward a transcendence that will never be fully grasped?

And as philosophers and painters struggle with these questions, Francis Drake sails into Plymouth harbor on September 26th, completing the second circumnavigation of the globe. His ship, the Golden Hind, is stuffed with Spanish treasure. Elizabeth will knight him on deck, inaugurating England's challenge to Iberian dominance of the seas.

Drake's voyage is the other face of 1580: not philosophical doubt but imperial confidence. The same Europe that produces Montaigne's exquisite uncertainty is conquering the world.

The essay form Montaigne invents embodies this tension. It offers no final answers, only the record of a mind in motion. It acknowledges contradiction as the basic condition of being human. And it insists that the examined life — not certainty, not dogma, but patient self-scrutiny — is the only genuine wisdom.

Three centuries later, Nietzsche, Emerson, and Virginia Woolf will all claim Montaigne as their ancestor. The personal essay, the stream of consciousness, the philosophical autobiography — they all begin here, in a tower near Bordeaux, with a man brave enough to ask: What do I know?
