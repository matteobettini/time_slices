The year is four seventy-six. In Ravenna, a boy sits on a throne he never asked for.

Romulus Augustulus — "little Augustus" — is perhaps fourteen years old. His father, the general Orestes, put him there ten months ago in a palace coup. Now Orestes is dead, killed by Germanic soldiers who wanted land he refused to give. And their leader, Odoacer, has come for the boy.

But Odoacer does something unexpected. He doesn't kill the child. He doesn't even claim the purple for himself. Instead, he packs the imperial regalia — the crown, the robes, the symbols of five centuries of Roman rule — and sends them east to Constantinople.

The message is clear: the West no longer needs an emperor of its own.

Historians will later call this the fall of the Roman Empire. But stand in Ravenna in four seventy-six and you wouldn't feel an earthquake. The tax collectors still collect. The law courts still judge. The aqueducts still flow. Odoacer even styles himself a Roman official, ruling in the name of the distant Eastern Emperor.

What changes is the symbol. After nearly five hundred years, no one in the West will again be called Augustus.

But empire was already becoming something else. Walk through Ravenna's Baptistery of Neon, completed just years earlier, and see Christ baptized under a dome of gold and blue. The mosaics don't depict earthly power — they depict heaven. The body flattens, the background glows, the soul matters more than the flesh. This is art for eternity, not for politics.

In Gaul, Bishop Sidonius Apollinaris writes elegant Latin letters to a world where his audience is increasingly Frankish and Visigothic. In Athens, the philosopher Proclus compiles Neoplatonic theology, systematizing how the soul ascends to the One. Classical culture continues — just not in Rome.

Julius Nepos, the legally recognized emperor, still lives in Dalmatia. He'll be assassinated in four eighty. The last Western emperor will spend his final decades in a villa near Naples — perhaps the estate of the legendary general Lucullus — pensioned off by the barbarian who took his crown.

Romulus Augustulus vanishes from history. But Rome doesn't fall. It transforms. The roads still run. The letters still flow. The mosaics still gleam. What looks like an ending is actually a metamorphosis — the ancient world becoming the medieval, one quiet change at a time.

The last emperor was a child. The empire that outlived him was made of words, laws, and light on gold.
