Fifteen thirty-two. The year power shows its many faces.

In Rome, printers finally release a book that has been circulating in manuscript for nineteen years. Niccolò Machiavelli wrote The Prince in fifteen thirteen, hoping to win favor with the Medici. He failed. He died in fifteen twenty-seven, never seeing it in print. Now, five years after his death, his explosive treatise reaches the world.

The Prince shatters every rule of political writing. Where medieval mirrors for princes taught rulers to be virtuous and trust in God, Machiavelli teaches something different. The prince must learn when not to be good. He must master virtù—the cunning, force, and flexibility to dominate fortune's wheel. It is better to be feared than loved, he writes, if you cannot be both.

This is political realism—the birth of modern political philosophy. Analyzing power as it actually works, not as moralists wish it would.

Yet in England, the opposite drama unfolds. On May fifteenth, the clergy submits to King Henry the Eighth: all church law now requires royal assent. The next day, Thomas More resigns as Lord Chancellor. More—the man who wrote Utopia, who imagined a perfect society—cannot endorse the king's supremacy over the Church. His conscience will not bend. Within three years, his conscience will cost him his head.

Machiavelli asks what works. More asks what is right. History will execute More and immortalize Machiavelli.

Meanwhile, in London, a German painter named Hans Holbein seeks new patrons. His old friends—including Thomas More himself—have fallen from favor. Among the merchants of the Steelyard, he finds commissions. His portrait of Georg Gisze becomes a masterpiece of Northern exactitude: every letter, every coin, every carnation rendered with hallucinatory precision. The motto on the wall reads "No joy without sorrow." Holbein survives where More will not—by adapting.

In Lyon, François Rabelais publishes Pantagruel under an anagram of his name—a satirical novel about a giant whose tongue serves as an umbrella, in whose mouth entire civilizations thrive. This is Renaissance humanism turned carnivalesque: the same love of ancient learning that produced Erasmus's scholarship here produces Rabelais's scatological laughter. Giants eating, drinking, urinating floods. The body triumphant.

And across the Atlantic, on November sixteenth, a different kind of giant falls. Francisco Pizarro ambushes the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca. One hundred sixty-eight conquistadors massacre thousands of unarmed attendants and capture an emperor whose ransom will fill a room with gold—six thousand kilograms of it, plus eleven thousand kilograms of silver.

It's not enough. Pizarro will execute Atahualpa anyway.

The logic of The Prince, written in Italy, enacted in Peru. The cunning and force Machiavelli theorized, applied with terrifying efficiency to an empire that had never seen a horse or heard a gun.

Fifteen thirty-two pivots on power—how to seize it, how to refuse it, how to laugh at it. Machiavelli codifies acquisition. Pizarro enacts it. More resigns from it. Rabelais mocks it. Holbein adapts to it.

And in a room in Cajamarca, gold piles toward the ceiling while an empire dies.
