Nineteen fifty-six. The year the edifices began to crack.

In February, Nikita Khrushchev stands before the Twentieth Party Congress in Moscow and does the unthinkable: he denounces Stalin. The man worshipped for three decades as a living god is exposed as a paranoid butcher, his purges and his cult of personality condemned by his own successor. Delegates weep. Some reportedly suffer heart attacks. Within months, the speech leaks to the West. The Thaw begins.

But Khrushchev's confession triggers demands he cannot control. In October, Hungarian students march through Budapest demanding free elections. The uprising spreads. Imre Nagy announces Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. For a moment, it seems the impossible is happening.

Then, on November fourth, Soviet tanks roll into the city. Twenty-five hundred Hungarians die. Two hundred thousand flee. The West watches, preoccupied with its own imperial folly: Britain and France invading Suez, then retreating humiliated under American pressure. Two empires exposed in a single month.

In Montgomery, Alabama, a different kind of victory. After three hundred eighty-one days of boycott, the Supreme Court declares bus segregation unconstitutional. Rosa Parks's refusal becomes law.

Meanwhile, art's old order is dying. On August eleventh, Jackson Pollock crashes his car on Long Island, drunk at the wheel. Forty-four years old. Abstract Expressionism's high priest, destroyed by the alcoholism that consumed his drip technique. The same year, in London, Richard Hamilton creates a tiny collage: a muscle man, a nude woman, a television glowing, every surface branded with consumer logos. Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing? Pop Art is born from magazine clippings, mocking what Pollock's heroic gestures tried to transcend.

In San Francisco, Allen Ginsberg's Howl howls from pocket-sized City Lights editions. I saw the best minds of my generation destroyed by madness. In London, John Osborne's Jimmy Porter rages against everything in Look Back in Anger. Kitchen sink realism replaces drawing-room comedy.

And in Paris, Camus publishes The Fall. A judge-penitent confessing his own hypocrisy while condemning everyone who listens. After The Stranger's indifference and The Plague's solidarity, now this: the anatomy of bad faith itself.

Nineteen fifty-six. The year of cracks. In ideology, in art, in the illusion that the postwar settlement was stable. The sixties are already germinating in the ruins.
