Sixteen twenty-three. The year of immortality in folio.

In a London printing house, two actors are making a bet against time. John Heminges and Henry Condell have gathered every text they can find — quartos, playhouse manuscripts, scraps of memory — and they are building a monument.

William Shakespeare has been dead for seven years. Half his plays have never been printed. Macbeth. The Tempest. Twelfth Night. Julius Caesar. Without this book, they would vanish — dissolved like the actors who played them, scattered like the audiences who wept and laughed.

The First Folio appears in November. About seven hundred fifty copies. Thirty-six plays. A young engraver named Martin Droeshout, who almost certainly never saw Shakespeare alive, creates the only portrait we have. Ben Jonson writes the preface: He was not of an age, but for all time.

Meanwhile in Rome, another man is making a different wager. Galileo Galilei publishes Il Saggiatore — The Assayer. His argument about comets is wrong, but his deeper claim is revolutionary: Philosophy is written in this grand book, the universe. It is written in the language of mathematics.

Where scholastics read Aristotle to understand nature, Galileo reads nature directly. Triangles. Circles. Geometric figures. This is how the cosmos speaks. And it speaks to anyone willing to learn its grammar.

The book is dedicated to the new pope, Maffeo Barberini — Urban the Eighth — elected on August sixth after a contentious conclave. Urban is a humanist, a poet, an admirer of Galileo. The Barberini bees stamped on the title page suggest that science and Rome might be friends.

On the same day Urban was elected, a thousand miles north, the Protestant cause collapsed. At Stadtlohn in Westphalia, Tilly's Catholic League army destroyed Christian of Brunswick's forces. Six thousand dead. Four thousand captured. All their artillery lost.

The Palatinate phase of the Thirty Years' War ends here — not with negotiation, but with annihilation. Frederick the Fifth, the Winter King of Bohemia, abandons hope. Maximilian of Bavaria takes his electoral vote. The war will continue for another twenty-five years.

And in Clermont, France, on June nineteenth, a baby is born. Blaise Pascal. In forty years he will invent probability theory and ask whether reason or faith offers the safer wager on eternity.

How does meaning survive? Print preserves Shakespeare. Mathematics preserves nature's truths. But some ideas fail to preserve themselves at all — like the dream of Protestant Germany, bleeding out on a Westphalian field.

Sixteen twenty-three. The Folio. The Saggiatore. The battle. The birth. A year when two roads to immortality — through art and through knowledge — briefly cross.
