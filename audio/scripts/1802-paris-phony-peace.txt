Eighteen oh two. The year of the phony peace.

On March twenty-fifth, Britain and France sign the Treaty of Amiens—the first peace in nine years. It will last exactly fourteen months. Everyone knows this. Napoleon uses the breathing room not to rest but to consolidate. By August, a plebiscite confirms him Consul for Life.

At the Salon of eighteen oh two, British visitors crowd into the Louvre for the first time in a decade. What they see is startling. Anne-Louis Girodet has unveiled Ossian Receiving the Ghosts of French Heroes—a phantasmagoric vision where spectral generals float through phosphorescent mist toward a legendary bard. Their forms dissolve into vapor. Girodet trained under Jacques-Louis David in strict neoclassicism. But this? This is neoclassicism becoming Romanticism. Reason yielding to dream. Napoleon has it hung at Malmaison. He sleeps beneath it.

And Napoleon is making other kinds of peace too. The Concordat with the Pope brings the Catholic Church back to France—but tamed, serving the state. François-René de Chateaubriand's Genius of Christianity appears at exactly the right moment: five volumes defending the faith not through theology but through aesthetics. Gothic cathedrals. Medieval romances. The sublime in scripture. Christianity, he argues, is simply more beautiful than Enlightenment reason. This becomes propaganda, of a sort.

But Germaine de Staël refuses to play along. Her novel Delphine tells of a brilliant woman destroyed by social convention. Napoleon sees himself in its critique of tyranny and intolerance. He will exile its author.

At Jena, Hegel and Schelling launch the Critical Journal of Philosophy. Hegel's essay Faith and Knowledge attacks the entire previous generation—Kant, Jacobi, Fichte—for leaving subject and object hopelessly sundered. He's preparing something. The Phenomenology of Spirit is still five years away.

Meanwhile, Napoleon signs the Law of May twentieth, reinstating slavery in French colonies. In June, Toussaint Louverture is tricked into arrest in Saint-Domingue. He will die in a French prison within the year. But the Haitian Revolution continues without him—and will succeed, precisely because Napoleon's troops die of yellow fever while trying to reimpose bondage.

Everyone knows the contradictions are unsustainable. The peace is phony not just diplomatically but philosophically. Religion returns—but domesticated. Art celebrates heroes—but as ghosts. Philosophy seeks unity—but must first destroy its fathers. And liberty for whites means slavery restored for Blacks.

Eighteen oh two. The last breath before the storm.
