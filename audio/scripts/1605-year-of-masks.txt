Sixteen oh five. The year everyone pretends to be something they are not.

In a Madrid bookshop, a new kind of story appears. Miguel de Cervantes, fifty-seven years old, a former soldier, a former slave, a failed playwright, has written something no one quite knows how to classify. Don Quixote de la Mancha reads too many tales of chivalry. He convinces himself he is a knight-errant. He rides out to right wrongs, rescue maidens, battle giants. The giants are windmills. The maidens are peasant girls. His armor is held together with cardboard.

And yet — this madman is also the sanest character in the book. His delusions reveal the world's cruelty more clearly than any realist could. Cervantes has invented the modern novel: fiction that knows it is fiction, that makes a reader's relationship to stories its central subject.

In London, William Shakespeare is writing King Lear. Another old man who cannot tell flattery from truth, who divides his kingdom based on pretty speeches and discovers too late which daughter actually loved him.

In Rome, Caravaggio completes Death of the Virgin. The monks who commissioned it are horrified. This is not how holy death is supposed to look. The Virgin's feet are swollen. Her head lolls. The mourners seem ordinary, their grief common. Rumor says the model was a drowned prostitute. True or not, the scandal is the point: Caravaggio has stripped sacred subjects of their sacred masks.

That same year, three popes wear the papal tiara. Clement the Eighth dies in March. Leo the Eleventh is elected, serves twenty-six days, then dies himself. Finally Paul the Fifth takes the throne. Even the Vicar of Christ cycles through identities.

And in England, on the fifth of November, Thomas Knyvet discovers a man named Guy Fawkes guarding thirty-six barrels of gunpowder beneath Parliament. The Gunpowder Plot aimed to assassinate King James, blow up the House of Lords, install a Catholic monarch. Fawkes, like Don Quixote, believed himself a holy warrior. His cause was as doomed as the knight's charge against windmills.

Masks upon masks. A knight who is a madman. A prostitute who is the Virgin. A nobody who claims to be Ivan the Terrible's murdered son — False Dmitry, crowned Tsar of Russia with Polish support, his real identity still unknown.

Francis Bacon publishes The Advancement of Learning. He argues for a new kind of knowledge — not contemplation for its own sake, but inquiry that improves human life. Even truth must shed its ornamental mask and prove its usefulness.

The seventeenth century begins by asking: what lies beneath the performance?

And whether anything does.
