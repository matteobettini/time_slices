Florence, fourteen-oh-one. Seven sculptors stand before a panel of judges in a city ruled by wool merchants and bankers. The prize: bronze doors for the Baptistery of San Giovanni, where every Florentine child is christened.

Each competitor must cast a single scene — the Sacrifice of Isaac. Abraham raises his knife. The angel intervenes. Show us what you can do.

Two finalists emerge. Lorenzo Ghiberti, twenty-three years old, elegant, technically flawless — his Isaac kneels with classical grace, every muscle anatomically perfect. Filippo Brunelleschi, twenty-four, dramatic, passionate — his Abraham twists with desperate violence, the knife already descending.

The judges deliberate. And then — they cannot decide. A tie.

Ghiberti claims victory. Brunelleschi refuses to share the commission. He will not collaborate with his rival. And so, rejected, he abandons sculpture entirely.

It is the best loss in the history of art.

Brunelleschi turns to architecture. He travels to Rome, measures ancient ruins, and returns to Florence with an impossible idea: a dome for the cathedral that has stood roofless for a century because no one knows how to cover such a span. Thirty-five years later, his dome rises — the largest masonry dome ever built, without traditional scaffolding, a technical miracle that will define the city's skyline forever.

Meanwhile, Ghiberti spends twenty-one years on the baptistery doors. When he finishes, Florence immediately commissions a second set. These he completes in fourteen fifty-two — the doors Michelangelo will call the Gates of Paradise.

But the story isn't just about two sculptors.

That same year, Geoffrey Chaucer dies in London, his Canterbury Tales unfinished — twenty-four of one hundred twenty planned stories, pilgrims frozen mid-journey. In Paris, Christine de Pizan, the first professional woman writer in Europe, defends women against centuries of misogyny. In Avignon and Rome, two popes excommunicate each other's followers while theologians debate who has the authority to judge between them.

Everywhere, the same question echoes: who decides?

In Florence, thirty-four judges pick between rival artists — competition replacing patronage. In Paris, scholars argue that councils outrank popes — consent replacing divine right. Even Chaucer's pilgrims vote on whose tale is best.

The medieval certainties are cracking open. One pope, one emperor, one truth — fracturing into pluralism.

Florence's baptistery doors don't just depict Isaac's sacrifice. They inaugurate a new covenant: that genius must be tested, authority must be earned, and the future belongs to whoever can convince the judges.

Both competition panels survive. You can see them today in the Bargello Museum — six hundred years later, still arguing their case.
