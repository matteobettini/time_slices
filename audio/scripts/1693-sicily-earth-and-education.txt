1693. Earth and Education.

On January eleventh, the ground shattered eastern Sicily. The Val di Noto earthquake — magnitude 7.4, the most powerful in Italian history — killed sixty thousand people and leveled fifty towns. Catania lost two-thirds of its population in four apocalyptic minutes.

Then something remarkable happened: from the rubble, Sicily reinvented itself. The Spanish Viceroyalty ordered comprehensive rebuilding, and local architects created Sicilian Baroque — flamboyant, theatrical, uniquely playful. Grinning masks and putti adorn facades. Balconies curve outward on wrought-iron flourishes. Churches crowned with elaborate belfries rise on cascading staircases.

Eight towns rebuilt in this style now form a UNESCO World Heritage Site — described as the culmination of Baroque art in Europe. Catastrophe became Sicily's greatest artistic achievement.

In England, John Locke publishes Some Thoughts Concerning Education. Building on his tabula rasa theory, Locke argues that children arrive with minds like blank slates — no innate ideas, no predetermined virtue or vice. "Nine parts of ten of what men are, good or evil, useful or not, comes from their education." If minds are blank, then education — not birth, not inherited status — determines character. Revolutionary implications for social reform.

Meanwhile, Edmond Halley — who funded Newton's Principia from his own pocket — applies mathematics to mortality itself. Using demographic records from Breslau, he constructs the first scientific life table: age correlated with probability of death. Insurance, pensions, demography: entire industries trace their foundations to Halley's 1693 paper. Death becomes calculable.

The threads converge on mortality and malleability. Halley transforms death into probability distributions; Sicily transforms mass death into architectural rebirth. Locke shows minds can be shaped; the Baroque cities rising from rubble show societies can be redesigned. Education shapes character; architecture shapes civilization.

The seventeenth century ends violently — but its intellectual legacy shapes the Enlightenment to come.
