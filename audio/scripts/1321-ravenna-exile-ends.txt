September, thirteen twenty-one. Ravenna.

A man who hasn't seen his home city in nineteen years lies dying. Dante Alighieri, exiled from Florence, condemned to burn if he ever returns, has just completed the greatest poem in the Italian language — a language he essentially invented.

The Divine Comedy is finished. Fourteen thousand two hundred thirty-three lines of terza rima, describing a journey through Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise. It is the first major work of European literature written not in Latin, but in the vernacular — the Tuscan dialect of Florence. Within a generation, that dialect will become Italian.

Dante's crime was politics. In thirteen-oh-two, the Black Guelphs seized Florence with papal backing and convicted their rivals in absentia. Dante, a prior of the White Guelphs, was fined, then condemned to death by fire. He wandered Italy for almost two decades, a guest of noble courts, writing his masterpiece in exile.

The poem is everything. It's medieval theology rendered as architecture — Aquinas's Summa transformed into verse. Hell descends in circles, each sin placed according to Aristotelian ethics. Purgatory climbs toward redemption. Paradise spirals through the heavens. But it's also confession, autobiography, political invective. Popes writhe in Hell. Florence is damned and mourned and yearned for in the same breath.

Back in that condemned city, Giotto di Bondone is painting St. Francis in the Bardi Chapel. The same artist painted Dante's portrait, possibly from life. Both men are doing something similar: turning from the stylized and hieratic toward observed reality. Giotto's saints bleed and grieve. Dante's sinners speak in recognizable voices.

The interlocking rhyme scheme Dante invented — terza rima — drives the poem forward like nothing before. Each tercet reaches for the next, chained together, relentless. It sounds like speech, but it moves like music.

Three years after Dante's death, William of Ockham will be summoned to Avignon for heresy. The scholastic synthesis Dante celebrated — faith and reason harmonized — will begin to crack. Nominalism will dissolve the universal into particulars. What Dante built as a cathedral of order, later centuries will deconstruct.

But in September thirteen twenty-one, the architect has finished his work. He dies in Ravenna. Florence will beg for his bones for centuries. The Franciscan monks will hide them, even brick them into a wall.

The exile never goes home. But every Italian speaker reads his language.
