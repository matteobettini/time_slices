Eighteen seventy-four. The year art stopped asking permission.

April fifteenth, Paris. In a photographer's abandoned studio on the Boulevard des Capucines, thirty artists open their own exhibition. No jury has approved them. No academy has blessed them. They've simply rented the space and hung their paintings.

The establishment is not impressed. Ten days later, critic Louis Leroy publishes his verdict in Le Charivari. He pauses before a canvas by Claude Monet — a harbor scene, all blurred ships and orange sun dissolving into mist — and sneers: even wallpaper in its embryonic state is more finished than this... this Impression.

The name sticks. The artists — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Pissarro, Berthe Morisot, Cézanne — are Impressionists now. An insult becomes an identity.

What unites them isn't just technique, though the technique is radical: broken brushwork, outdoor painting, light dissolving form. What unites them is refusal. They don't want to reform the Salon. They want to ignore it. To make their own space, their own rules, their own public.

They're not alone in this rebellion. In England, Thomas Hardy publishes Far from the Madding Crowd, inventing Wessex — a fictional country mapped onto the real Dorset, where his vision will unfold on his terms. In Germany, Friedrich Nietzsche publishes Schopenhauer as Educator, attacking the universities as factories of conformity. True education, he writes, comes through encounter with exceptional individuals who liberate you to become yourself.

Meanwhile in Milan, Giuseppe Verdi conducts his Requiem in the church of San Marco. It's a tribute to Alessandro Manzoni, the novelist who had died exactly one year before. Verdi wrote it not for commission, not for performance, but because he loved the man.

And in a house in Bayreuth, Richard Wagner finishes the music for Götterdämmerung — the twilight of the gods. He's building his own theater now, where his operas will be performed exactly as he envisions them.

Everywhere, creators are building their own institutions. The Impressionists rent Nadar's studio. Nietzsche imagines a fellowship of the untimely. Wagner raises a festival hall. Hardy invents a county.

Louis Leroy meant Impressionism as mockery. The artists took the name anyway. That's the spirit of eighteen seventy-four: turning the insult into a manifesto, the rejection into a badge of honor.

Art stops asking permission. Art just begins.
