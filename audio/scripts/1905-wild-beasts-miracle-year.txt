Paris, October nineteen-oh-five. The Salon d'Automne is scandalizing the city.

In Room Seven, visitors stop and stare — not in admiration, but in shock. Henri Matisse has painted his wife with blotches of green and pink where her face should be. André Derain's landscapes explode in colors that exist nowhere in nature. A critic named Louis Vauxcelles sees a classical sculpture surrounded by these violent canvases and makes a joke that will stick: "Donatello among the wild beasts."

Les Fauves. The wild beasts. The name is meant as an insult. The artists adopt it as a badge of honor.

This is the moment color breaks free from description. Matisse isn't trying to show what his wife looks like — he's showing what it feels like to look at her. The Impressionists captured light; the Fauves capture emotion.

Four months earlier, in Dresden, four architecture students founded a group called Die Brücke — The Bridge. They aim to connect all the revolutionary elements in art. Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Erich Heckel, Karl Schmidt-Rottluff — names that will define German Expressionism. They don't know about Matisse yet. The movements emerge independently, within months of each other, attacking convention from both sides of the Rhine.

But the real revolution happens in a Swiss patent office.

Albert Einstein is twenty-six years old. He reviews applications for inventions all day, then goes home and rewrites the universe. In June, he explains the photoelectric effect — light behaving as particles, not just waves. In July, Brownian motion — proving atoms exist. In September, special relativity: there is no absolute space, no absolute time. The speed of light is the only constant, and everything else bends around it.

And then, almost as an afterthought, three pages: E equals m c squared. Mass and energy are the same thing. The foundation for nuclear physics, written by a man the scientific establishment had rejected for every academic position he'd applied for.

Meanwhile, in St. Petersburg, the old order is bleeding.

On January twenty-second, a peaceful procession of workers marches to the Winter Palace. They carry icons and portraits of the Tsar. They want only to petition Nicholas the Second for better conditions. The Imperial Guard opens fire. Hundreds die. Bloody Sunday.

The Russian Revolution of nineteen-oh-five erupts. Strikes paralyze the empire. Workers form councils — soviets — to organize themselves. By October, a general strike forces the Tsar to promise civil liberties. It won't last. But the soviets will be remembered. Twelve years later, they'll return.

What connects a patent clerk in Bern, wild beasts in Paris, expressionists in Dresden, and revolutionaries in St. Petersburg? Nothing direct. Einstein didn't know Matisse. Kirchner had never heard of the soviets. Husserl, developing phenomenology in Göttingen, worked in isolation from them all.

What connects them is simpler and stranger: the simultaneous discovery that the old certainties were lies.

Absolute space? A fiction. Color as description? A prison. The Tsar as father? A murderer. Objective truth? A construction.

The nineteenth century ended not in nineteen hundred, but here, in nineteen-oh-five — the year the wild beasts roared, the workers rose, and a quiet man in Switzerland proved that even time itself was relative.

The twentieth century had finally begun.
