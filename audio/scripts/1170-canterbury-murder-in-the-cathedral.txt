The year is 1170. In Canterbury Cathedral, four knights draw their swords at vespers.

Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury, stands before the altar as the armed men burst through the cloister door. They have ridden hard from the king's court, convinced they know Henry the Second's will: rid England of this turbulent priest.

Becket refuses to flee. He refuses even to defend himself. The first blow cuts open his scalp; the final one scatters his brains across the cathedral floor. A witness records that one knight, placing his foot on the archbishop's neck, says: "He won't be getting up again."

The quarrel was jurisdictional. Who judges criminous clergy — royal courts or Church courts? The Constitutions of Clarendon, which Becket rejected, would have made priests answerable to the king. Six years of exile in France, a fragile reconciliation, then this: swords in a sanctuary.

But murder transforms everything. Within two years, pilgrims are already streaming to Canterbury; within three, Pope Alexander canonizes Becket as a saint. Henry himself will walk barefoot to the shrine and submit to flogging by the monks. The Church wins what it could never have won in argument.

Yet the same decade that creates a martyr also creates new kinds of speech. In Lyon, a merchant named Peter Waldo commissions the first vernacular translation of the New Testament — Scripture in the language of the people, not the Latin of priests. His followers, the Poor of Lyon, will be condemned as heretics, but their movement survives to join the Protestant Reformation four centuries later.

In Champagne, Chrétien de Troyes writes Érec et Énide at the court of Marie, daughter of Eleanor of Aquitaine — the first Arthurian romance in French, inventing a literature of courtly love that will shape Western imagination for centuries.

In Paris, Notre-Dame rises slowly on the Île de la Cité. Maurice de Sully's cathedral perfects the Gothic style: pointed arches, flying buttresses, walls that dissolve into glass. Where Romanesque churches were fortresses, Gothic cathedrals become cages of light — theology made architectural.

Connecting these worlds stands John of Salisbury: he studied under Abelard, served as Becket's secretary, and witnessed the murder. His Policraticus theorizes the limits of royal power; he ends his career as Bishop of Chartres, patron of the cathedral schools. In one life, the conflicts of the twelfth-century renaissance converge.

The threads run deeper. Becket died defending the Church's monopoly on sacred interpretation. Waldo attacked the same monopoly from below, putting Scripture in laymen's hands. Chrétien translated legend from Latin chronicle to French entertainment. Even Notre-Dame's vast windows serve a textual purpose: they will tell biblical stories in glass for those who cannot read.

Who controls the sacred text? The century is discovering that the answer cannot be singular. The vernacular is awakening. Kings learn that martyrs are more dangerous than rebels — and priests learn that Scripture, once spoken in common tongues, cannot be unspoken.

The year is 1170. Four knights butcher an archbishop. And the words that will undo medieval certainties are already being written in new languages, by men who have never met, across a continent still convinced that Latin alone can hold the truth.
