Two hundred and fifty. The year the Roman Empire begins to doubt itself.

In January, Pope Fabian becomes one of the first victims of Emperor Decius's persecution — the first systematic campaign against Christians across the entire empire. Decius is convinced that neglecting the old gods has brought plague and military disaster upon Rome. His solution: force everyone to sacrifice and obtain a certificate proving their loyalty.

Christians face an impossible choice. Burn incense to the gods, and betray everything you believe. Refuse, and face torture, execution. Or flee, abandoning your community. Thousands choose martyrdom. Thousands more — the lapsi, the fallen ones — find ways to survive. Some sacrifice. Some bribe officials for fake certificates.

Meanwhile, in a house on the Aventine Hill, a philosopher teaches a different kind of escape.

Plotinus arrived in Rome six years earlier, after barely surviving a catastrophic military expedition to Persia. Now he gathers senators, women, and young philosophers to hear his lectures on what will become known as Neoplatonism.

His teaching is radical. All reality emanates from a single source — the One — utterly simple, beyond even being itself. Through contemplation and moral discipline, the soul can ascend back through layers of existence toward mystical union with this divine source. Four times during these years, Plotinus himself achieves such union. Philosophy becomes not just thought, but practice. Spiritual exercise.

He never mentions Christianity. But he offers pagans their own path beyond the crumbling empire.

In Caesarea, another philosopher-theologian sits in prison. Origen, author of the most sophisticated defense of Christianity the ancient world would produce, has been arrested and tortured. He will die of his injuries three years later. Remarkably, Origen and Plotinus once studied under the same teacher: the mysterious Ammonius Saccas of Alexandria.

The art of the age captures this existential tension. The Ludovisi Battle Sarcophagus, carved around this time, shows Romans triumphant over barbarians — but the central figure, a young general, gazes outward with strange serenity, untouched by the carnage. His eyes seem to look beyond the battlefield entirely.

Two fifty. An empire in crisis. Bodies demanded to kneel before old gods. Souls teaching themselves to fly.
