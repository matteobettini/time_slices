Eighteen oh eight. The year the nations awaken.

On the second of May, Madrid erupts. French cavalry charges through cobblestone streets while Spanish citizens fight back with knives, paving stones, whatever they can grab. Napoleon thought he'd swallowed Spain whole — instead, he gets what he'll later call his "Spanish ulcer."

The executions come the next morning. Hundreds of rebels lined up against a wall on Príncipe Pío hill. Francisco Goya is somewhere in Madrid during all this. Six years later, he'll paint what he saw — or imagined — in canvases that abandon every heroic convention. Just the terror. Just the dying.

Meanwhile, in occupied Berlin, a philosopher named Johann Gottlieb Fichte stands before an audience and delivers fourteen lectures that will change history. The Addresses to the German Nation. Prussia has just been humiliated at Jena. French soldiers patrol outside. And Fichte argues that the Germans possess a unique spiritual mission — that nations aren't just political arrangements but organic communities with their own souls.

This is philosophical nationalism being born. Dangerous? Absolutely. But also — people everywhere are waking up to the idea that they belong to something larger than a dynasty.

In Weimar, Goethe finally publishes his masterpiece. Thirty years he's been working on Faust. The scholar who sells his soul for experience, who declares that "In the beginning was the Deed." The eternal striver who seduces, destroys, and keeps reaching for more. The Romantic hero — or anti-hero — who refuses every limit.

And in Vienna, on a freezing December night, Beethoven premieres both his Fifth and Sixth Symphonies in a single exhausting concert. Four hours of music in an unheated theater. The audience sits in overcoats. But those four notes that open the Fifth — fate knocking at the door — become the sound of human will against cosmic indifference.

The Enlightenment promised universal reason. Eighteen oh eight delivers something messier: peoples claiming their own destinies, artists depicting horror without glory, philosophers arguing that nations have souls. The age of nationalism begins — with all its grandeur and all its coming catastrophes.
