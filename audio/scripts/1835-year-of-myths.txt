Eighteen thirty-five. The year of myths.

It begins in February, when a Finnish doctor named Elias Lönnrot publishes the Kalevala — thirty-two poems assembled from oral traditions gathered across Finnish and Karelian villages. This is not transcription. This is creation. Lönnrot shapes fragments into an epic that will give Finland a national identity before it has a nation.

In May, a Danish storyteller named Hans Christian Andersen publishes four tales for children. The Tinderbox. The Princess and the Pea. Critics ignore them. Andersen will write a hundred and sixty-seven more.

Meanwhile, a twenty-seven-year-old German theologian named David Friedrich Strauss begins publishing something far more incendiary: The Life of Jesus, Critically Examined. He does not call the Gospel miracles lies. He calls them myths — imaginative expressions of religious truth created by early Christian communities. Zurich offers him a professorship, then rescinds it amid riots. His effigy is burned. Biblical scholarship will never be the same.

In September, aboard HMS Beagle, Charles Darwin arrives at the Galápagos Islands. He spends five weeks observing finches and tortoises — data that will gestate for twenty-four years before producing a theory that makes myth of Genesis itself.

And in Paris, Alexis de Tocqueville publishes the first volume of Democracy in America. He doesn't celebrate democracy — he dissects it. He describes how equality liberates and threatens simultaneously. How the tyranny of the majority manufactures consent while generating anxiety.

At the Royal Academy, Turner exhibits The Burning of the Houses of Parliament — architecture dissolving into pure atmospheric fire. He had watched it burn, sketched it from a boat on the Thames. The painting chooses impression over documentation. Myth over record.

Caspar David Friedrich paints The Stages of Life — five ships at varying distances, five figures contemplating mortality. That June, a stroke will cripple his ability to paint in oils.

The Romantics believed in myth. They revered it, sought it, tried to revive it.

The generation of eighteen thirty-five begins doing something different. They study how myths are made. Lönnrot assembles fragments into epic. Strauss explains how religious communities transform historical figures into sacred ones. Tocqueville analyzes how democracy manufactures its own legitimacy. Darwin — though he doesn't know it yet — is gathering evidence that will replace one creation myth with another: natural selection.

Even Andersen's fairy tales, supposedly for children, are full of death and longing and transformation — the archetypal patterns of human experience dressed in modern clothes.

This is the hinge. Between Romanticism and positivism. Between reverence and analysis. Between believing in myths and understanding how they work.

Eighteen thirty-five. The year mythmaking became a science — and science began making new myths.
