Seventeen thirty-eight. The Year of Revivals.

In October, near Naples, workers begin digging through twenty metres of solidified volcanic debris. They're looking for marble to decorate a palace. What they find instead is Herculaneum — a Roman city frozen in time since Vesuvius buried it in seventy-nine AD. For sixteen centuries it lay forgotten. Now statues emerge from darkness, bronzes of philosophers, carbonised scrolls, a library preserved in volcanic glass. The excavation will transform European art. Within decades, the playful curves of Rococo will give way to Neoclassical severity — columns, togas, republican virtue. The ancient world is returning.

In Paris, Voltaire publishes his Elements of Newton's Philosophy — written with his collaborator and lover Émilie du Châtelet, a mathematician who will later produce the definitive French translation of Newton's Principia. France still believed in Cartesian vortices, in a cosmos of invisible whirlpools pushing planets around the sun. Voltaire offers something different: gravity, empirical science, observation over authority. "Minerva dictated, and I wrote," he acknowledged. The book converts France from Descartes to Newton — and makes science a weapon against superstition.

On May twenty-fourth, in a small meeting room on Aldersgate Street in London, an Anglican priest named John Wesley hears someone reading from Luther's preface to Romans. At about a quarter before nine, he records in his journal: "I felt my heart strangely warmed. I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation." The Aldersgate experience transforms intellectual conviction into personal assurance. Wesley will carry this fire across Britain and America, founding Methodism — religion as felt experience, not institutional form.

And in May, another debut: a twenty-eight-year-old Lichfield schoolmaster publishes his first major poem. Samuel Johnson's London attacks the capital in two hundred sixty-three lines of Juvenalian fury — corruption, venality, a city where "all crimes are safe, but hated poverty." Alexander Pope reads it and asks who wrote it. Told it was an obscure unknown, Pope replies: "He will soon be déterré" — unearthed.

That word captures the year. Excavation — literal and metaphorical. At Herculaneum, workers dig through volcanic rock to recover a buried past. Voltaire excavates Newton from English insularity. Wesley excavates primitive Christianity from beneath Anglican coldness. Johnson excavates Juvenal to attack Hanoverian corruption.

The Enlightenment advances not by inventing the new, but by remembering what was forgotten. The bronzes of Herculaneum, the equations of Newton, Luther's Romans, Juvenal's Third Satire — each is a buried treasure whose recovery disrupts the settled order. Charles the Seventh at Naples, Voltaire at Cirey, Wesley at Aldersgate, Johnson in Grub Street — four figures digging up the past to transform the present.

Seventeen thirty-eight. The year Europe began to remember.
