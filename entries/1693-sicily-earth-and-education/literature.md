**William Congreve**, at twenty-three, storms onto the London stage with two plays that define **Restoration comedy**. In March, *The Old Bachelor* opens at Drury Lane to enormous success — **John Dryden** himself revised the script and declared the young playwright "the best Comic Poet living." In October, *The Double Dealer* follows, darker and more satirical. Congreve's wit is surgical: "Hell hath no fury like a woman scorned" and "you must not kiss and tell" originate here. His comedy dissects aristocratic manners, marriage as transaction, and the gap between social performance and authentic feeling. Actress **Anne Bracegirdle**, who would become his muse, plays lead roles. Though Congreve would only write for seven more years before leaving the stage, his influence on satirical comedy of manners would prove permanent.

In Paris, **Madame de La Fayette** dies in May, leaving behind a revolutionary legacy. Her *La Princesse de Clèves* (1678) — published anonymously — invented the **psychological novel**, prioritizing the interior experience of a conflicted woman over external action. The Princess's refusal of passion in favor of duty, her confession to her husband of an attraction she resists — this was fiction that explored consciousness itself. La Fayette's salon had connected her to **La Rochefoucauld** (author of *Maximes*, who died in 1680) and **Racine**; her work demonstrated that prose fiction could achieve the psychological depth of tragedy.

The **Ladies' Mercury** appears in London — just four issues, February to March — but its significance is enormous: the **first periodical specifically for women**. Published by **John Dunton**'s Athenian Society, it promises to address "all the most nice and curious questions concerning love, marriage, behaviour, dress and humour of the female sex." Though brief, it inaugurates the women's magazine as a genre. The Female Tatler and Female Spectator would follow.

**John Dryden**, England's foremost poet (though recently stripped of his laureateship for refusing to convert from Catholicism after the Glorious Revolution), publishes *Examen Poeticum*, an anthology, and *A Discourse Concerning the Origin and Progress of Satire* — critical prose that establishes principles of English poetic form. In Japan, the "floating-world" novelist **Ihara Saikaku** dies, having created fiction depicting the merchant class and pleasure quarters with unprecedented realism. His death marks the apex of the *ukiyo-zōshi* genre.
