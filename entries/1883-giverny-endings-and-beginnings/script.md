# 1883: Endings and Beginnings

## Art

On **April 20th**, **Claude Monet** moves into a rented house in Giverny, a village on the Seine sixty kilometers northwest of Paris. He will live there for forty-three years, until his death in 1926. The famous water garden doesn't exist yet—that comes later, after he buys the property in 1890—but the light is already perfect. Monet is forty-two, established but restless, Impressionism's most dedicated practitioner still chasing the impossible: to paint not things but the light that touches them.

Eleven days later, **Édouard Manet** dies in Paris at fifty-one, his left leg amputated eleven days before from locomotor ataxia caused by syphilis. Manet was never quite an Impressionist—he always wanted Salon acceptance, always worked in his studio—but he made them possible. *Olympia*, *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe*: these scandals of the 1860s cracked open the door through which Monet, Renoir, and the others walked. The teacher dies; the students scatter to their gardens.

In Brussels, **Les XX** forms in October, a group of twenty artists who will show the avant-garde: Rodin, Whistler, later Seurat and Van Gogh. **Renoir** is painting dancers—*Dance at Bougival*, *Dance in the Country*—his figures fuller now, more classical, pulling back from pure Impressionism toward something solid. Art is between movements, the revolutionary energy of the 1870s spent, the next rupture not yet visible.

## Literature

Two children's books appear in 1883 that will outlast most "serious" literature of the decade. In February, **Carlo Collodi**'s *The Adventures of Pinocchio* completes its Italian serialization and appears in book form—a puppet who wants to be a real boy, darker and more moralistic than later adaptations suggest, a fable of transformation written for a nation still learning to be itself (Italy unified only thirteen years earlier).

In May, **Robert Louis Stevenson**'s *Treasure Island* appears in book form after magazine serialization. It invents modern pirate mythology: the treasure map marked with X, the one-legged sailor with a parrot, the desert island, the ambiguous villain you can't help admiring. Long John Silver is the year's most memorable character—charming, treacherous, somehow sympathetic, a study in moral complexity disguised as adventure.

**Mark Twain** publishes *Life on the Mississippi*, his memoir of piloting steamboats before the war, one of the first major books submitted to a publisher as typescript rather than manuscript. In August, **Ivan Turgenev** dictates his last story from his deathbed at Bougival—the same village where Renoir set his dancers. The Russian novel's great generation is passing: Dostoevsky died in 1881, Turgenev now, Tolstoy still writing but turning from fiction to prophecy.

## Philosophy

**Karl Marx** dies in London on **March 14th**, stateless and nearly penniless, outlived by only two of his seven children. Eleven mourners attend the funeral at Highgate Cemetery three days later. **Friedrich Engels** delivers the eulogy, declaring that Marx "discovered the law of development of human history" as Darwin discovered the law of organic nature. The claim is grandiose, but Engels isn't wrong about influence: within thirty-five years, Marx's ideas will inspire the Russian Revolution; within seventy, half the world will live under governments claiming his authority.

The same month Marx dies, **Friedrich Nietzsche** writes the first part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* in ten days, in a creative explosion at Rapallo on the Italian coast. The prophet Zarathustra descends from his mountain to announce that God is dead and the Übermensch must be born. Marx promised collective liberation through historical process; Nietzsche promises individual transformation through will. The two philosophies will haunt the twentieth century, twisted by followers neither man would recognize.

**Thomas Hill Green**, the Oxford idealist who brought Hegel to English philosophy, dies in March at forty-five. His *Prolegomena to Ethics* appears posthumously. Philosophy is turning from pure speculation toward social concern—Green's students will become reformers, his liberalism shaping British welfare policy.

## History

On **May 24th**, the **Brooklyn Bridge** opens after fourteen years of construction—the longest suspension bridge in the world, connecting Manhattan and Brooklyn across the East River. President Chester Arthur walks across; 150,000 people follow on opening day. The bridge is engineering as monument: its Gothic towers speak to cathedral ambitions, but its steel cables are pure industrial confidence. A week later, a stampede sparked by collapse rumors kills twelve people. The future arrives, but not without casualties.

On **October 22nd**, the **Metropolitan Opera** opens in New York with Gounod's *Faust*—not because the city needed another opera house, but because new money (Vanderbilts, Morgans) was excluded from old money's boxes at the Academy of Music. Culture as class warfare, even in the Gilded Age.

And in August, **Krakatoa** explodes. The eruption on **August 26-27** is among history's largest, audible three thousand miles away in Australia, generating tsunamis that kill over 36,000 people. But the long-term effect is stranger: volcanic ash circles the globe, scattered into the stratosphere, and for years afterward, sunsets burn red worldwide. Global temperatures drop. The sky itself changes color. Some scholars believe Edvard Munch's blood-red sky in *The Scream* (painted 1893) remembers Krakatoa's atmospheric aftermath—nature itself making art possible.

## Connections

The year's theme is **succession**—endings that are also beginnings, deaths that clear space for what comes next. Marx dies and Nietzsche rises to replace materialist revolution with spiritual transformation; both thinkers obsess over how humanity might overcome its current condition, though they imagine opposite paths. Manet dies and Monet gardens at Giverny; the provocateur who broke the Academy gives way to the patient observer who will spend forty years painting the same pond. Wagner dies in February and the Metropolitan Opera opens in October; the total artwork yields to the institution.

Even the literature enacts transformation: Pinocchio wants to become a real boy; Jim Hawkins in *Treasure Island* must become a man by confronting Long John Silver's seductive wickedness. Krakatoa's eruption literalizes the theme—the island destroying itself to birth new land, new sunsets, new possibilities for how we see.

The Brooklyn Bridge performs the same logic as infrastructure. It doesn't just connect two cities; it makes a new thing possible—the modern metropolis, the commuting worker, the separation of where you live from where you work. Fourteen years of construction, twenty-seven workers dead, and finally a structure that will outlast everything built in 1883. Some endings open onto futures the dying cannot imagine.
