The **troubadours** reach their zenith. **Bertran de Born**, baron of Hautefort, composes *sirventes* celebrating the joy of war — *"I tell you that I find no such savor in eating butter and sleeping, as when I hear cried 'On them!' and from both sides hear horses neighing through their head-guards"* — while his love songs circulate through Occitan courts. When Richard and Philip delay departing for crusade, Bertran chides them in verse praising Conrad of Montferrat's heroic defense of Tyre. Poetry and politics intertwine; the troubadour is diplomat, propagandist, war correspondent.

In the north, **Chrétien de Troyes** has recently died, leaving *Perceval* unfinished — the first appearance of the **Grail** in literature, the quest that will obsess European imagination for centuries. His romances transform Arthurian legend from chronicle to psychological drama: knights whose adventures are interior, love that conflicts with duty, the court as laboratory of feeling. Continuators will complete Perceval, adding thousands of lines, but the master's touch is gone.

**Marie de France**, writing at the Anglo-Norman court, composes her *Lais* — short narrative poems drawing on Celtic legend, exploring women's perspectives on love with unprecedented sophistication. *Lanval*, *Yonec*, *Bisclavret* — her protagonists are often women constrained by marriage, seeking agency through the supernatural. She also produces fable collections and the *Espurgatoire Seint Patriz*, adapting Latin sources into French vernacular. The woman poet claims her voice.

In the Islamic world, **poetry** remains the supreme art. Arabic panegyrics praise Saladin; Persian verses circulate from Khorasan to Egypt. The *adab* tradition — literary cultivation encompassing poetry, prose, conversation, and conduct — defines courtly culture from Cordoba to Cairo.
