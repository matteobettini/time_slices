The great cathedrals continue rising. At **Chartres**, reconstruction proceeds after the fire of 1134, with the west facade and Royal Portal already standing — their elongated column-statues of Old Testament kings a new sculptural naturalism. At **Notre-Dame de Paris**, begun in 1163, workers complete the choir and perfect the system of **flying buttresses** that will transform Gothic architecture: thrust externalized so walls can become windows, stone dissolved into frames for light. This is architecture as theology — the church as *heavenly Jerusalem*, luminosity as divine presence.

In the **Byzantine world**, artistic traditions persist despite political fragmentation. **Monreale Cathedral** in Norman Sicily, consecrated in 1182, displays perhaps the greatest fusion of Byzantine and Latin art: 6,340 square meters of gold-ground mosaics covering every surface, Christ Pantocrator gazing down from the apse, Old and New Testament narratives spiraling through the nave. The mosaics are executed by Greek craftsmen working for Norman kings — a visual translation between civilizations.

Meanwhile, **Islamic art** flourishes under the Ayyubids. **Saladin** himself commissions architectural projects in Cairo and Jerusalem, including the **Citadel of Cairo** begun in 1176. The style is austere compared to Fatimid exuberance — geometric precision, inscribed calligraphy, the arabesque as meditation. Artistic exchange flows through crusader contact: Western artists encounter Persian metalwork, Syrian glass, Egyptian textiles. The traffic goes both ways — Byzantine silk reaches Baghdad, while **Kufic script** appears on European church vestments.
