In **Fustat** (Old Cairo), **Moses Maimonides** nears completion of his *Mishneh Torah* — fourteen volumes systematizing all of Jewish law, the most comprehensive halakhic code ever compiled. He writes in Mishnaic Hebrew, not the Aramaic of the Talmud, seeking clarity over obscurity. His thirteen principles of faith distill Judaism's theological core. Soon he will begin *The Guide for the Perplexed* (1185-1190), reconciling Aristotelian philosophy with Torah revelation. Maimonides serves as physician to Saladin's vizier — a Jewish philosopher embedded in the Ayyubid court, healing Muslim bodies while shaping Jewish minds.

In **Córdoba**, **Averroes** (Ibn Rushd) has been writing his comprehensive commentaries on Aristotle since 1169 — the work that will earn him the title "The Commentator" in medieval Europe. Now serving as qadi (judge) and soon to become court physician, he argues that philosophy and revelation cannot truly conflict: where scripture seems to contradict reason, allegory reveals the hidden meaning. His *Tahafut al-Tahafut* (Incoherence of the Incoherence) defends philosophy against al-Ghazali's attacks. Within years, the Almohad caliph will turn against him, burning his books — but Latin translations will carry his Aristotle into the heart of Christian scholasticism.

These two men — **Maimonides** and **Averroes** — share Córdoba as birthplace, Aristotle as master, the same crisis: how to reconcile ancient Greek reason with revealed religion. Both produce syntheses that will shape their traditions for centuries. Both are refugees — Maimonides fled Almohad persecution; Averroes will face it soon. The **translatio studii** flows through Islamic lands: Greek texts preserved in Arabic, translated into Hebrew and Latin, philosophy moving across religious boundaries even as armies clash.
