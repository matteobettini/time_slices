The paradox of 1191: **civilizations clash on the battlefield while sharing a philosophical language**. Maimonides serves Saladin's court; Averroes synthesizes Aristotle for caliphs who will soon burn his books; Richard massacres prisoners while sending gifts to Saladin's brother al-Adil. The crusade is holy war — and it is also, inescapably, contact. Crusaders encounter Damascus steel, Arabic medicine, Greek texts preserved in translation. The **translatio studii** moves through conquest as well as commerce.

**Bertran de Born** celebrates war while the troubadour tradition he embodies — courtly love, refined sentiment, vernacular poetry — owes debts to Arabic *zajal* and *muwashshah*. The fin'amor that structures Chrétien's romances echoes the *'udhri* love poetry of Arabia. Cultures that fight with swords trade in metaphors.

The **architectural revolution** connects to the same synthesis. Flying buttresses solve an engineering problem — how to build higher, thinner, lighter — but they express theology: light as divine presence, the church as heaven on earth. Meanwhile, Islamic architecture achieves its own transcendence through different means: the muqarnas ceiling, the geometric arabesque, space dissolving into pattern. Both traditions reach for the infinite using mathematics.

**Maimonides' thirteen principles** attempt what the crusades cannot achieve: clarity, consensus, systematic truth. But his rationalism generates controversy — some Jews reject his philosophical method as alien, Greek, corrupting. **Averroes** faces the same accusation from Muslim traditionalists. The Aristotelian synthesis promises to reconcile faith and reason; it also provokes reaction, persecution, the burning of books. Philosophy crosses borders; so does backlash.
